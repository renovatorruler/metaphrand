# How the Story Engine Works

*An essay for listening. About ten minutes read aloud.*

## The one idea

A television writers' room does not trust the writer. It trusts the board.

The board is the wall of index cards where the story gets broken before anyone writes a page. In a working room, a story exists in a chain of forms. First cards on a wall. Then an outline. Then a script. Then the cut. At every step, the new form is checked against the form before it, and notes are given against that earlier form, never against an older version of itself. Nobody says "this scene is worse than your last draft." They say "this scene broke the outline."

Our engine is that room, rebuilt in code. It has to be, because our writer is a language model, and a language model has one deep flaw that no reminder can fix. Left alone, it slides toward the most expected version of whatever it writes. The average scene. The safe choice. The professional-sounding sentence that says nothing. Craft advice does not survive contact with that gravity. So the engine does not remind the writer to be good. It makes the bad version fail to build.

Everything below is one chain. A story enters as an idea and leaves as a finished film, and at every joint there is a gate. Some gates are code that blocks. Some are a judge with a fixed rubric. One is a human, and that one outranks all the others.

## Stage one. The ground truth

Before any scenes exist, the story writes its constitution. Seven or eight short artifacts, and every later decision is checked against them forever.

The premise, in a strict grammar borrowed from the playwright Lajos Egri: this leads to that. For our moon story, faith kept fifty years past its object leads to one fatal act of keeping.

The central dramatic argument, which must be a claim someone could argue against. A country is its promises. And, crucially, the other side's best case gets written down too, because a theme with no real opposition is a sermon.

A designing principle, the one line that makes this telling original. A last stand told as a cookout.

The lighthouse scene, an idea from the screenwriter Tony Gilroy. Before structure, you write the one scene the whole movie exists for, in prose, and every later scene has to prove it belongs to the same animal.

The contested object. The hero and the opponent must want the same thing, and the constitution has to state, in writing, why both cannot have it. If you cannot write that sentence, they can both win, and there is no story.

And the opponent's plan, written as a campaign that would succeed even if the hero never showed up. A villain who only reacts to the hero is the hero being kept safe.

Why does all this matter? Because of the deepest bug we found in machine writing. A model revises against its own previous draft. It patches the patch, and its errors ride along forever, and eventually it writes sentences that only make sense to its own past self. The constitution kills that. Revision never argues with yesterday's draft. It argues with the ground truth.

## Stage two. The spine

Now the story becomes a chain of beats, and here the type system starts doing real work.

Every beat connects to the one before it through exactly one of two words: therefore, or but. This happens, therefore that happens. This happens, but that blocks it. The third word, and then, does not exist in the system. There is no way to write it. That rule comes from the South Park creators, who told a class of film students that if the words "and then" fit between your beats, your story is dead. In our engine that sentence is not advice. A beat that can only honestly be joined by "and then" has no legal encoding, so it cannot be written down at all.

Each beat also answers the two questions the Breaking Bad room asked of every index card. What is this character trying to get? What is standing against them? And each beat carries two numbers. The hero's fortune, from minus five to plus five. And the stakes, from one to ten.

Those numbers turn safety into something measurable. The fortune curve must actually move, must reach real depths late in the story, and must not sit flat. The stakes may never shrink. And at the climax, the gate takes roll call: the antagonist must be present and acting. If nobody is opposing the hero in the final scene, the build fails.

We proved this on our own material. The first outline of the moon story, the one that felt weak, failed this gate ten separate ways. The buddies were parked safely on a radio. The threat evaporated before the ending. Nobody stood against the hero at the cookout. The rebuilt spine passes, and the difference between those two outlines is the difference the whole engine exists to enforce.

## Stage three. The world and the cast

A story is a populated world, not a corridor with a hero walking down it.

So the cast gets built as a web, not a list. Principals are placed on opposed poles, so conflict is structural before anyone speaks. Each character gets a natal chart, an astrological profile we use as a random seed for personality, and from the chart comes a voice card: how this person's sentences move, what they refuse to say, where their grammar breaks. Each also gets an iceberg backstory, which informs behavior on the page and is never stated on it.

Two gates stand here. The first is preflight: production physically refuses to start while any of those artifacts is missing or missing a cast member. The day we turned it on, it caught three real gaps in our own current story, including a main character who had never been given a voice card. The second is blind attribution: strip the names off all the dialogue and make a judge guess who said each line, using only the voice cards. If the judge cannot tell two characters apart, they are one character wearing two shirts, and they go back for rework.

## Stage four. Permission to exist

Before a scene may be written, it presents a card.

The card answers David Mamet's three questions. Who wants what from whom? What happens if they do not get it? Why now? It adds Aaron Sorkin's requirement of a formidable obstacle, one that could actually win. It records the scene's opening truth and its closing truth, which must differ, and the value at stake, whose charge must flip. It writes down what the audience learns, and the question the scene throws forward to the next one. An incomplete card does not compile. A scene that cannot fill the card in does not get written, which is exactly the fate the dead scenes of earlier drafts deserved.

Alongside the card sit the layer contracts, which protect a scene's soul during revision. Each scene records what it shows, what it deliberately withholds, what it hints, and what must stay off the page entirely. An edit that states the buried thing is illegal even if every sentence in it is lovely. And the intrigue builder schedules the story's secrets: hint early, reveal late, one mystery chain at a time, every reveal opening a new question until the last one.

## Stage five. The clean room

Here is the strangest rule, and maybe the most important one.

The writer that drafts the scene is never allowed to see the conversation about the scene.

A language model never leaves the meeting. Its blank page sits at the bottom of the chat transcript, so whatever it writes becomes a continuation of the conversation. Ask for a design after an hour of notes and you get minutes of the meeting wearing the design's clothes. It will explain reverted decisions to an audience that was never in the room. The cure is separation. Notes flow into the spec. Then a fresh writer, with empty memory, receives only the spec: the constitution, the card, the voice cards, the house rules. It cannot leak the meeting because the meeting does not exist in its world.

Two more things happen at this stage. Entropy gets injected on purpose: one variable of the scene, the setting, the way in, the wrong detail, gets knocked off the expected center before writing starts, because signature lives off center and no later polish can add it. And every render leaves a receipt, so the finished text can prove it came from the pipeline and not from someone typing around it.

## Stages six and seven. Substance, then finish

The finished draft faces two batteries, in strict order.

The substance battery asks whether this is the right scene at all. Is it a fight or a postcard? Does it change both the situation and a person, which is the scene doctor Jim Mercurio's test? Does the heart ledger balance? That last one deserves a word: relationships in this engine are ledgers. Warmth banked early, in passing, in scenes about something else. A reconciliation must spend a deposit that was banked earlier, because you cannot argue anyone warm, and kindness that costs the giver nothing counts for nothing. The battery also measures flesh against bone, because a story stretched over nothing but plot beats is shrink-wrapped, and a real world has people whose lives do not orbit the hero.

Then a human reads it, and the human is the stop. Structure locks on approval.

Only after the lock does the finishing battery touch the language, and it may touch nothing else. It kills the machine tells. Sentences arranged for effect. Meaning rationed out in dramatic fragments. The little essay reflexes, the in-conclusion and the it-should-be-noted and the let's-dive-right-in. The rule of three used as a drumbeat. The reach for the poignant closing line, which is the most reliable machine tell of all. Scenes end flat, on a fact.

## Stage eight. Production

The last stage is the studio floor. One law rules it: no raw text ever reaches a synthesizer. Dialogue passes through a sealed performance layer that re-checks every word against the approved script, attaches the acting notes, and only then speaks. Sound is mixed the way films are mixed, in stems, dialogue and atmosphere and effects and music, ducked and balanced into one master. Everything expensive is cached, because the fastest render is the one you never repeat.

## What the audit found

We drew this whole machine as a diagram recently and audited it honestly, and two findings are worth saying out loud.

First, the chain really is one spine, not a patchwork. The same scene law shows up three times, at the card, at the substance read, and in an advisory skill, and that repetition is deliberate: permission, judgment, advice. One law, three doors.

Second, for a while the middle of the chain was law without a bailiff. An earlier purge of old code deleted the programs that enforced the middle stages, and the rules survived only as documents. Documents do not block. We have since recovered every one of those enforcers from history and rebuilt them inside the type system, and the first thing they did was catch real gaps in our own story. The remaining debt is wiring: a gate only exists where it is invoked, so every entry point has to actually call them.

That is the machine. A constitution nothing may contradict. A spine that cannot say "and then." A cast that must prove its voices apart. Scenes that ask permission to exist. A writer with no memory of the meeting. Two batteries in the right order. A human who outranks everything. And at the very end, four old men light a grill on the moon, and the engine's whole job was making sure that when they do, it means something.
