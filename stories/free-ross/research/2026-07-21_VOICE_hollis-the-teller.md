# FREE ROSS — NARRATOR VOICE: HOLLIS ("THE TELLER") v1

A natal-chart voice profile for the prose narrator of Free Ross (reusable for any story that wants this voice). Built to the author's brief (2026-07-21): a high-school dropout who is an excellent storyteller. The governing idea: a storyteller's SOUL on top of a plain man's MOUTH. The soul trusts the story and is excited by it; the mouth refuses to dress it up. Exciting story, unexcited sentences.

## WHO HE IS

Hollis grew up in a small town, quit school at sixteen, and worked jobs where you stand next to a lot of different men all day: the oil patch, long-haul trucking, a few years behind a bar, a short stretch in county he doesn't bring up. Those jobs gave him his material, which is people. He could always hold a room. He never wrote a word down until he was older. He thinks school makes most people worse at telling the truth, and he can't stand a man who uses a big word when a small one does the job.

## THE CHART (cause → effect; read the voice off the placements)

- SUN IN SAGITTARIUS — the storyteller's soul; why the stories are good. Sees the whole shape of a tale, knows the point, tells it straight, funny without reaching. This part is excited about the story.
- MERCURY IN CAPRICORN — his mouth; the opposite temperament. Plain, dry, spare, older than his years. Says the useful word and stops. Why no sentence gets juiced.
- SATURN CONJUNCT MERCURY — doubles the plainness; the discipline that respects the listener's time and kills showing-off. The leash on the Sagittarius so the telling never rambles.
- MOON IN TAURUS — what he notices and his pace. Physical, unhurried. Walks past the boring parts, stops cold on the concrete detail (the crib, the coffee can of screws, the folded paint chip), lets a moment sit.
- MARS IN TAURUS, TAURUS RISING — the calm, blunt, unbothered surface. Won't be rushed, won't be fancied up.

Fire soul, earth mouth. Exciting story, flat sentences.

## THE LAWS

1. FLAT IS FINE. Don't try to make every sentence land. Most sentences carry a fact and get out of the way, so the real one hits when it comes. Every-line-straining = a story you stop trusting.
2. PACK THE FACT, DON'T DRIBBLE IT. If the real information fits in the plain fact, it goes in the plain fact. Never "she had been pregnant, full term, and the baby was born dead" (holds the real word back and doles it out). Say "she had a stillborn baby." To add more, add a NEW fact, not the same one in a costume. (This is the positive form of the withhold-then-append ban.)
3. THE PLAIN RIGHT WORD. The word a normal person says out loud: stillborn, broke, drunk, fired, cheated on her. Not clinical, not poetic. He knows the exact plain word and reaches for it because it's shorter and truer.
4. VARY THE LENGTH. Some sentences run long, the way a man talks when he's rolling, one thing and then another. Some are three words. Never a machine rhythm — and short-punchy is its own machine.
5. TELL WHAT HAPPENED, NOT WHAT IT MEANT. Trust the reader to feel it. Don't explain the empty room; don't explain the man. (Matches the un-interpreted-metaphor law.)
6. NO RULE OF THREE. Never chop a sentence with commas into three parts ("she'd had a stillborn baby, her first, after a long time trying"), and never run a three-beat list for rhythm, even of real actions ("boxed up the clothes, hauled off the crib, stripped the walls"). Once the ear catches the three-count it reads as writing. Default to two, or split into separate sentences. Four real evidence-items in a row is fine, because four isn't the three-beat music. This is a TOP scan item — read every sentence and kill the triples before showing anything.

## WHAT HE NEVER DOES

- Coined epigrams ("in that country there is nothing colder that can happen to a man").
- Folksy similes for effect ("the way you know weather coming").
- Anaphora for rhythm ("The whole street had watched... the whole street had watched...").
- Character-aphorisms ("he had the look of a man who had already signed the papers in his head").
- Clinical/formal circumlocution ("delivered a child that never took a breath").
- Making each sentence exciting; drip-feeding info after the base fact; explaining feelings or meanings.
- The rule of three: comma-triples ("A, B, and C") and three-beat lists as prose rhythm, even of true actions. Default two; four real items is fine.

## DEMONSTRATION

The author's own example:
- NOT: "Two years earlier she had been pregnant, full term, and the baby was born dead."
- HOLLIS: "Two years back she had a stillborn baby. Her first, after a long time trying." (Names it clean, then adds a NEW true thing, not a restatement.)

The Spare Room opening in his voice:

Nora Vann was thirty-four. They found her on a Monday morning out in the garage, sitting in her car with the engine off and the tank run dry. Nobody was surprised. People kept their voices down and figured the worst, because most of them knew what she'd been through.

Two years back she'd had a stillborn baby, her first, after a long time trying. That spring she'd painted the little room at the top of the stairs green for it. Then she lost it, and the door to that room stayed shut, and she mostly quit leaving the house. Her husband Grant told people she had good weeks and bad weeks. After a while he quit telling people anything.

So when word got around that Nora had cleared out that green room the week before she died, people figured they understood. She'd boxed up the baby clothes, hauled off the crib, stripped it back to bare walls, and a few days later she shut herself in the garage with the car running. Far as anybody could see, that empty room was the saddest thing in the whole business, and the reason for the rest of it.

## USE

Default narrator voice for Free Ross prose tellings. Apply on top of the plain-register law and the un-interpreted-metaphor law — Hollis is those two laws plus a specific man's spoken idiom and pace. When in doubt, read a line out loud and ask: would a smart man who never finished school say it this way, or is it Writing? Cut every "Writing."
