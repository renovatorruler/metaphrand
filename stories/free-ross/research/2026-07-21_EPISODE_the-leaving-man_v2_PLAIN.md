# FREE ROSS — "THE LEAVING MAN" v2 — the plain walkthrough (flip-centered)

Supersedes v1 and the v1 plain walkthrough. Center is now the metaphorical flip (the empty grain elevator), not the "Ross gets tricked" trap. Currency is dollars. The climactic stake is load-bearing per the system correction.

## The opening

The show opens on a farm at sunrise. A tall grain elevator stands against an empty sky. At its base lies an old man, dead. His name is Otis Brewer, seventy years old, and it was his own equipment. Everyone assumes the obvious thing: a broke old farmer, the bank about to take the place, climbed up at dawn and jumped. The county is gentle about it. The insurance company is quietly pleased, because a policy pays double for an accident and pays almost nothing for a suicide, so they have every reason to call it a suicide.

## Why everyone believes it

There is one fact the whole county agrees on, and it is the thing that settles the matter in everyone's mind. The day before he died, Otis emptied his grain elevator. He sold off every bushel he had stored. Everyone reads it the same way, and so does the audience: a ruined old man cashing out, closing his accounts, emptying himself of his last possessions before he climbed up and ended it. An empty elevator is an empty man. It is the saddest, most obvious thing in the world, and it is why nobody looks any closer.

## Ross gets hired

Otis's widow is grieving and needs the accident payout, so her insurance agency hires Ross to prove it was an accident. A simple job. But Ross keeps circling that emptied elevator, because it bothers him, and he asks the one question nobody asked: where did the money go?

## The flip

The answer turns the whole story inside out. Otis took every dollar from that grain and immediately poured it into next year. Seed, fertilizer, inputs for a crop he meant to plant in the spring. He did not empty the elevator to cash out. He emptied it to make room. Because to a farmer an empty granary is not an empty life. It is a swept, ready, waiting thing, cleaned out so it can take in the next harvest. Otis was not a man giving away his last possessions before he died. He was a man clearing the deck to go all in on next year.

And that is the exact opposite of a suicide. You do not bet everything you have left on next spring the night before you decide there will be no next spring. The audience watched that empty elevator for half the episode and read it as a grave. Now they see it was a future. The case does not just shift, it inverts. This was never the story of a man who lost hope. It is the story of a man who had just found a way to win, and a man about to win something that another man was counting on him losing is a man somebody needs dead.

## What the flip sends Ross to find

The flip is a compass, not proof. It tells Ross the story is wrong and points him at a question: how was Otis planning to save the farm? Ross finds it. Otis had quietly signed a contract to grow a specialty crop for a buyer next season, and he had already taken the advance payment into his bank account. That contract was his way out. It would have saved the farm. And saving the farm would have destroyed something valuable to his business partner, a man named Dell, who held a private agreement to buy Otis's share cheaply the moment Otis went under. If Otis climbed back out of that hole, Dell's cheap buyout vanished. So Dell needed Otis to go under, permanently, before anyone learned there was a deal.

## The table, and a bet that actually holds

Now the poker-style hearing, where the two sides bet real money on the facts. The insurance company's whole theory needs one thing to be true: that Otis was a man in despair with no future. So that is exactly where Ross bets everything. He stakes on a single fact: that Otis had signed a contract for next season's crop and banked the advance. The insurance company cannot shrug this off, the way they could shrug off "he loved his dog." A man who took money to deliver a harvest next fall, two days before he supposedly jumped, is not a man without a future. If that fact stands, their suicide theory is dead. And they cannot deny it, because it is sitting in the bank record with his signature on the contract. The bet holds. The suicide story dies at the table, in public.

## The turn to murder

Killing the suicide theory forces the real question: if he did not jump, how did he end up at the foot of the elevator? Here is the detail that closes it. Otis was found with his safety harness unclipped, as if he had been careless. But the company's own wall is covered with thirty years of safety-award photos, and in every single one Otis is clipped in. Always. A man does not spend thirty years clipping in and then forget on the last morning of his life. Somebody put him at the base of that elevator. Dell knew Otis's plans well enough to make the death look like despair, but he did not truly know the man, and his staging was wrong by one lifelong habit. Now it is a murder claim with weight behind it, and the size of it is bigger than everything Dell schemed for. The award will run past everything he owns, and his agency will spend the rest of his working life collecting it out of him.

## The ending

Two quiet pictures. First, the office wall of thirty years of free promotional caps, one for each year, every one reading BREWER, including all the years Dell ran the place while the dead man's name stayed on the door. Nobody points it out. It is there for the drive home, so you understand why a man might come to hate a name enough to empty a future to get it. And last, the grain elevator at dusk, swept clean and empty and ready, the way Otis left it, waiting for a spring planting that someone made sure would never come.
