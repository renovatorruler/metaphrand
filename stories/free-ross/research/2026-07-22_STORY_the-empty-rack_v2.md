Quanah, TX

They found Earl Combs on his kitchen floor on a Thursday morning. He had been shot twice and the back door was standing open. The cash box he kept in the bedroom closet was gone, and so was the coffee can of silver dimes that always sat over the stove. Whoever did it left the TV, and they left a hand-tooled show saddle that was probably worth more than the truck in the yard.

By noon there were more people in Earl's yard than had visited him all year. Constable Dane stood on the porch and kept them off the steps.

"I'll tell you the sorriest part," Kelso said. He ran the gun shop in town. "Two weeks back he come in and sold me his whole collection. All 31 of them. Didn't even keep the .22."

"There it is then," Dane said. "Earl knew somebody was coming for him. He had that water fight going with the Dodd place, and a feud like that eats at a man. He saw it coming and it finally broke his nerve."

"Either that or he was fixing to do himself," somebody called from the fence line. "An old man clears the guns out of his house, he's getting ready one way or the other."

Dane nodded slowly. "Scared or done. Either way he knew."

That was the story everybody carried home from the yard, and by supper time most of the county believed it.

Dallas, TX

The Free Ross Detective Agency was above a transmission shop, and whenever the lift ran downstairs the coffee pot shivered a little. Mable sat at the front desk with the subscriber book open, drawing a line through a name.

"The Hendersons quit us," she said. "That's the third one this month."

"The Hendersons never had a claim in their life," Ross said from his desk. "People pay for coverage for years and nothing happens to them, so they decide nothing ever will. Then something happens."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"It's a business model."

The stairway door opened and a young woman came in carrying a little boy asleep on her shoulder. She stopped inside the door and looked around like she wasn't sure this was the right place. Honestly, the office had that effect on people.

"Ma'am," Mable said, and pulled a chair out with her foot.

"My name's Lacy Combs." She sat down carefully so the boy wouldn't wake. "My grandpa was Earl Combs, out in Quanah. Somebody shot him in his kitchen 2 weeks ago and took a cash box, and the constable is calling it a drifter robbery and waiting for it to blow over. I was told your outfit takes claims when the local law won't."

"We do, if there's somebody to claim against," Ross said. "A drifter with no name is a free man. Who do you say it was?"

"I don't know. That's the problem." She rubbed the boy's back without seeming to notice she was doing it. "But I know my grandpa wasn't scared of anybody, and I know he wasn't fixing to hurt himself, and the whole county has decided he was one or the other because he sold his guns. They say it at the co-op like it's already settled. Well, it's not settled."

Ross studied her for a moment. "Is the boy yours?"

"Judd. He's 4." She looked down at him. "We were moving in with Grandpa on the first of the month. I left my husband back in March, and Grandpa had been after me all spring to come home."

Ross wrote down the date and underlined it twice.

Quanah, TX

Kelso's shop smelled like solvent and old carpet. Earl's collection was racked along the back wall with paper tags on the trigger guards. The estate hadn't decided what to do with it yet.

"Buy-book," Ross said.

Kelso hauled a green ledger up onto the counter and spun it around. The Combs entry ran two full pages, every make and serial number in Kelso's careful handwriting, and at the bottom was the total: $19,400, paid by check.

"Did he dicker with you?"

"Earl? Dickered like a horse trader. Got me up from 17 flat." Kelso smiled at the memory, then seemed to remember why Ross was asking. "It wasn't a broke man's dicker, if that's what you're after. Broke men take the first number you say."

"Who else was in the shop when the collection came in?"

Kelso thought about it. "That was a Tuesday. Reyes came in for shells. The Miller kid put a deposit down on a deer rifle." He tapped the counter. "And Merl Dodd stood right about where you're standing while I entered it all in the book. Asked me if that was the whole Combs armory. I told him down to the .22. He said, well I'll be. Then he bought a box of primers and left."

Ross turned the ledger back around. "Has anybody local been spending silver dimes lately?"

Kelso's eyebrows went up. "Now that you mention it. Dodd's boy paid his feed tab at the co-op in dimes on Saturday. Sue Ellen was annoyed about it because she had to roll them all."

Out at the Combs place, Ross let himself in with Lacy's key. The church ladies had already cleaned the kitchen. He went through the house slowly, room by room, and he didn't touch much. In the hall closet he found a paper sack from the hardware store. Inside were cabinet latches, the kind you screw onto low cupboards so a child can't get into the bleach. There were two dozen outlet covers in the sack with them, and a small safety gate for the top of a staircase that was still in its shrink wrap. The receipt was dated the same day as the gun sale.

Ross stood in the hallway for a minute with the receipt in his hand. "There you are," he said to the empty house.

He called Mable from the phone at the co-op. "Everybody up here read it backwards," he said. "They see a man sell every gun he owns and they read fear. But a scared man doesn't sell his guns, Mable. A scared man buys more guns. Nobody in the history of Texas ever got scared and disarmed."

"So why did he sell?"

"Judd. He's 4 years old and he was moving in on the first of the month. Earl had 31 guns in that house and some of them stayed loaded, and grandbabies get their fingers into everything. He wasn't emptying the house. He was baby-proofing it. There's a sack of cupboard latches in the hall closet with the same date on the receipt as the gun sale."

Mable was quiet for a second. "That might be the sweetest thing anybody ever turned into evidence."

"Now here's the ugly part," Ross said. "One man stood at Kelso's counter and watched the whole collection go into the book, down to the .22. The same man has been feuding with Earl over water for two summers. And his boy just paid a feed bill in silver dimes."

****

The hearing was held in the back room of the Cattleman's Association, Judge Sikes presiding. He was retired but he still kept a docket for the panhandle counties. Dodd sat with the man from Panhandle Mutual, which carried him. Ross sat with Lacy. There was a chalkboard on the wall with two columns, CLAIM and ANSWER, and the stakes went up on the board in Sikes's handwriting as each side posted them.

Dodd's man laid out their answer first. "Merl Dodd had a water disagreement with the deceased. That's no secret and it's no crime either. But nobody in his right mind walks into Earl Combs's house at night. Every soul in this county knew Earl kept better than 30 guns in there, and he'd run off two prowlers in ten years. That house was a fort. Whoever went through the back door that night was a stranger who had no idea whose kitchen he was standing in. That's Merl's defense, and I'd say it's a good one."

"It is a good one," Ross said. "But it has one requirement hiding in it. Merl had to believe the guns were still in the house."

He wrote the agency's number on a slip and handed it up, and Sikes chalked it on the board: $40,000.

"That's on one fact," Ross said. "On the 9th of last month, Merl Dodd stood at Kelso's counter and watched Earl's whole collection get entered in the buy-book, and he asked if that was the armory entire, and he was told down to the .22. Kelso will swear to it under bond. If Merl knew that house was empty of guns, the fort defense is finished. If he wants to claim he didn't know, he can match my number and we'll put Kelso under bond and hear him out."

The Panhandle Mutual man leaned over and spoke into Dodd's ear for a while. Dodd stared at the chalkboard. Everybody in that room could do the arithmetic. Calling the stake meant Kelso testifying under bond, and Kelso had no earthly reason to lie, and three other men had been in the shop that same Tuesday.

They didn't call.

After that the rest of it came apart fast. The dimes went into evidence, and so did the co-op ledger. A tech from the vet clinic testified that Dodd had asked her, 2 days before the killing, whether Earl's old dog was still boarded there for its hip surgery. It was.

Sikes gave his award before supper. The claim against Merl Dodd was made out in full, the death and everything that flowed from it, and the number came to more than Dodd had or was ever going to have. Panhandle Mutual paid Lacy that same week. Then it opened a ledger of its own with Dodd's name on it, and a man his age would be working against that number for the rest of his life.

****

Lacy moved into the house on the first of the month anyway. She told Mable that Earl would have been mad at her if she hadn't.

The first night, after Judd was asleep, she went looking through the hall closet for spare sheets and found the paper sack instead. She sat down on the hallway floor and turned one of the little latches over in her hands for a long time. Then she went and got a screwdriver from the kitchen drawer and screwed the first latch onto the cupboard under the sink, where the bleach was.

She did the outlet covers in Judd's room next, working quietly by the lamp. She left the stair gate for the morning.
