# FREE ROSS — "THE SPARE ROOM" (plain register + Hollis voice, no rule-of-three; author-approved 2026-07-22)

Nora Vann was thirty-four. On a Monday morning they found her out in the garage, in her car. The engine was off and the tank had run dry. Nobody was surprised. People kept their voices down and figured the worst. Most of them knew what she'd been through.

Two years back she'd had a stillborn baby. It was her first, and they'd been trying for years. That spring she'd painted the little room at the top of the stairs green for it. Then she lost the baby. After that she kept that door shut and mostly quit leaving the house. Her husband Grant told people she had good weeks and bad weeks. After a while he quit telling people anything.

So when word got around that Nora had cleared out that green room the week before she died, people figured they understood. She'd hauled off the crib and stripped the room back to bare walls. A few days later she shut herself in the garage with the car running. Everybody figured that empty room was the saddest part of it, and the reason for all the rest.

Her insurance paid nothing if she'd killed herself, and a lot if it was an accident. Her sister lived two states away and hadn't gotten Nora on the phone in months. She didn't buy the easy answer. She hired the agency, and the agency sent Ross.

Grant kept the house clean. He walked Ross through it with his hands in his pockets and stopped at the bottom of the stairs. He wouldn't go up. He said that green room was the first thing that ever came between them, and it was the last thing too. He'd come home one day and found it empty, and after that he was sure she'd given up. He'd already made up his mind how it ended.

Ross went up alone.

The room at the top of the stairs was empty, but it didn't smell like a room that had been shut two years. It smelled like sanding dust and fresh primer. Somebody had filled the old nail holes and sanded them down. There was a line of blue painter's tape along the top of the baseboard. On the windowsill sat a fan of paint samples, and one was folded out and creased flat, a pale yellow with a thumbnail dug into the corner.

Ross looked at it a while. Then he went out to the garage, and he didn't look at the car. He looked over the shelves and the bins. Behind the ladder stood a long flat box, still taped shut, with a picture of a crib on the side. The receipt was tucked under the tape. It was dated the Thursday before she died.

On the workbench, under a coffee can full of screws, there was a little appointment card from a doctor's office. The date on it was the next Tuesday. She hadn't lived to Tuesday.

Ross put the card in his pocket and stood in that garage a long time.

Everything after that he found on paper, because a hunch is worth nothing at a hearing. Nora had been to the doctor three times in five weeks. She was nine weeks pregnant. She'd stopped taking a medication she'd been on for two years. It isn't safe once you're carrying, so her doctor had put her on something else. The new bottle was in her purse, in a pharmacy bag with her prenatal vitamins. Her old prescription hadn't been refilled since spring. The bottle of it in her bathroom was a spring bottle, still near full.

But her blood came back with that old drug in it, a full dose, from the night she died. She hadn't filled it or touched it since spring. She couldn't have taken it herself. Somebody put it in her.

Nora's father had built a company and left it to her. He left his brother, her uncle Walt, to run it till she wanted it. She never wanted it. Walt had run it eleven years by then and had come to treat it as his own. If Nora died with no child, the company rolled into a family foundation, and Walt ran the foundation. If she had a child, the company would pass to the child. The child would have guardians, and guardians open the books. Walt handled the family's insurance too, and paid Nora's medical bills out of the company account. Her clinic charges crossed his desk. He knew there was a baby coming before her own husband did.

At the hearing the other side wanted to put it to bed quiet, as a suicide, which is what the policy needed it to be. They had her sad history, and they had the empty room. It was a believable story, and most of the county already believed it.

Ross bet the agency's money on one fact and wouldn't come off it. Nora had stopped taking her medication in the spring and never took it again. He laid down the pharmacy records, the near-full bottle, the doctor's new list, the bag of vitamins. The other side couldn't shrug at it the way they'd shrugged at a sad woman and an empty room. Their whole story needed Nora to swallow those pills herself that night, and she couldn't have. They couldn't call it a lie either, because the pharmacy's own paper said so. They folded.

Once the room understood somebody else had put that drug in her, there was only one man it could be. The man who paid her doctor and knew about the baby before anyone. The man who stood to lose everything he'd called his own for eleven years, the day that child was born.

The claim came down on Walt for the whole of it, Nora and the child both. It was more money than he had or would ever have. His agency paid the estate, and then it started collecting it back out of Walt. He'd be paying it the rest of his life.

Nora's sister came and stayed a month. She finished the room. She primed the walls and painted them the pale yellow off the folded card. She brought the crib up from the garage and leaned it against the wall, still in its box.
