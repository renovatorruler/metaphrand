Nora Vann was thirty-four. On a Monday morning they found her out in the garage, in her car. The engine was off and the tank had run dry. Nobody was surprised. People kept their voices down and figured the worst. Most of them knew what she'd been through.

Two years back she'd had a stillborn baby. It was her first, and they'd been trying for years. That spring she'd painted the little room at the top of the stairs green for it. Then she lost the baby. After that she kept that door shut and mostly quit leaving the house. Her husband Grant told people she had good weeks and bad weeks. After a while he quit telling people anything.

So when word got around that Nora had cleared out that green room the week before she died, people figured they understood. She'd hauled off the crib and stripped the room back to bare walls. A few days later she shut herself in the garage with the car running. Everybody figured that empty room was the saddest part of it, and the reason for all the rest.

Nora had a policy. It paid nothing if she'd done it to herself, and it paid a lot if it was an accident. The company that wrote it wanted a suicide, and on what everybody could see, a suicide was an easy sell.

Her sister Carla lived two states off. She hadn't gotten Nora on the phone in months, and she carried that around with her. But she didn't believe the easy version. She said so to anybody who'd listen, and when nobody listened she spent her own money and hired the agency. The agency sent Ross.

Carla met him at a coffee place off the highway. She was older than Nora, and she had her sister's hands. She said Nora had cried on the phone at Christmas, hard, but people cry at Christmas. The last time they talked Nora had sounded lighter, and Carla had figured it for a good week and hadn't pushed. She said she'd give a lot now to have pushed. Then she said the thing she'd driven all that way to say. Nora didn't leave messes for other people to clean up. Pills in a car in a shut garage was a mess, and it wasn't her.

Ross told her that wasn't much to go on. Carla said she knew it wasn't. She paid for the coffee and left him the file.

Grant Vann kept a clean house. He walked Ross through it with his hands in his pockets and answered most of the questions before Ross finished asking them. He said the green room was the first thing that ever came between them, two years back. He said it was the last thing too. He'd come home one evening the week before and found it emptied out, and after that he was sure. When they got to the stairs he stopped at the bottom and didn't go up. He said he hadn't been up since. He'd made up his mind how it all ended, and what he wanted now was the money to bury her and be done.

Ross went up alone.

The room at the top of the stairs was empty, but it didn't smell like a room that had been shut two years. It smelled like sanding dust and fresh primer. Somebody had filled the old nail holes and sanded them smooth. There was a line of blue tape along the top of the baseboard, the kind you lay down before you paint. On the sill sat a paper fan of paint chips. One was folded out and creased flat, a pale yellow, with a thumbnail dug into the corner.

Ross stood there a while. Then he went out to the garage, and he didn't look at the car. He looked over the shelves and the bins. Behind the ladder stood a long flat box, still taped shut. A picture of a crib was printed on the side. The receipt was folded under the tape, dated the Thursday before she died.

On the workbench, under a coffee can full of screws, there was a card from a doctor's office. The date was written in blue pen. The next Tuesday. She hadn't lived to Tuesday.

Ross put the card in his pocket and stood in that garage a long time.

A hunch is worth nothing at a hearing, so after that he worked in paper.

Nora had been to the doctor three times in five weeks. She was nine weeks along. She'd been on the same medication better than two years and she'd stopped it cold, because it isn't safe once you're carrying. Her doctor had put her on something else, and that new bottle was in her purse in a pharmacy sack, sitting with her prenatal vitamins. The old prescription hadn't been filled since spring. The bottle of it in her medicine cabinet was a spring bottle, and it was near full.

Then the county's own tests came back. That old drug was in her blood the night she died, and plenty of it. A full dose. She hadn't filled it or touched it since spring. She couldn't have taken it herself. Somebody put it in her.

Nora's daddy built a company out of nothing and left it to her when he passed. He left his brother Walt to run it until Nora wanted the chair, and Nora never wanted the chair. Walt ran it eleven years. Somewhere in there he quit thinking of it as hers.

Here's how the money sat. If Nora died with no child, the whole thing rolled into a family foundation, and Walt ran the foundation. If Nora had a child, the child got the company. A child that age comes with guardians, and guardians go through the books. Walt handled the family's coverage on top of all that. He paid Nora's doctor bills out of the company account, so her charges crossed his desk. He knew there was a baby coming before Grant did.

The hearing was in a plain room with a felt-topped table. The other side came to put it to bed quiet, as a suicide. They had her history and her marriage. They had that empty room, and they laid it all out gentle and sad, and most of the county already believed every word.

Ross didn't argue the history and he didn't argue the room. He put the agency's money on one fact and wouldn't come off it. Nora quit her medication in the spring and never took it again. He laid it down piece by piece: the pharmacy record with no refill since April, the near-full bottle from her cabinet, the new prescription in her doctor's hand, the sack of prenatal vitamins.

The man for the other side said he didn't see what it changed. A sad woman does a sad thing.

Ross said it changed everything, because their whole story had Nora swallowing those pills herself that night. So put money on it. Say out loud that Nora Vann took her own medication the night she died, and stake it.

The man read the pharmacy paper for a while. He didn't put a dollar on it. You can't bet on a woman taking a pill she threw out in April, not with the drugstore's own record on the table in front of you. He folded.

Once the room took it that another hand had put that drug in Nora, the rest of it only pointed one way. It was the man who paid her doctor and knew about the baby before her own husband did. The same man who stood to lose everything he'd called his for eleven years, the day that child was born.

The claim came down on Walt for the whole of it, Nora and the child both. It was more money than he had or would ever have. His agency paid the estate, and then it turned around and started collecting it back out of Walt. He'd be paying it the rest of his life.

Carla came and stayed a month. She finished the room. She primed the walls and rolled them the pale yellow off the folded chip. She brought the crib up from the garage and stood it against the wall, still in the box.
