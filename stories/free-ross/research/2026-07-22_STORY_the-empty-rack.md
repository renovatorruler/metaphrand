# FREE ROSS — "THE EMPTY RACK" (Perseus voice; 2026-07-22)

Quanah, TX

They found Earl Combs on his kitchen floor on a Thursday morning, shot twice, the back door standing open. The cash box from his bedroom closet was gone. So was the coffee can of silver dimes he kept over the stove. The TV was still sitting there, and so was a hand-tooled show saddle worth more than the truck in the yard.

By noon there were more people in Earl's yard than had visited him in a year. Constable Dane stood on the porch keeping them off the steps.

"I'll tell you the sorriest part," said Kelso, who ran the gun shop in town. "Two weeks back he come in and sold me his whole collection. All thirty-one. Didn't keep so much as the .22."

"There it is, then," Dane said. "Earl knew somebody was coming for him. Man had that water fight with the Dodd place, feud like that eats at you. He seen it coming and it broke his nerve."

"Either that or he was fixing to do himself," somebody said from the fence. "Old man clears his house of guns, that's a man getting ready one way or the other."

Dane nodded slow. "Scared or done. Either way he knew."

That was the story that left the yard, and by supper it was the story of the whole county.

****

Dallas, TX

The office of the Free Ross Detective Agency was above a transmission shop, and when the lift downstairs ran, the coffee in the pot shivered. Mable was at the front desk with the subscriber book open, drawing a line through a name.

"The Hendersons quit us," she said. "Third one this month."

"The Hendersons never once had a claim," Ross said from his desk. "People pay for coverage for years, nothing happens to them, and they decide nothing ever will. Then something happens."

"That a comfort to you?"

"It's a business model."

The stairway door opened and a young woman came in with a little boy asleep on her shoulder. She stood there a second like she wasn't sure the office was an office.

"Ma'am," Mable said, and pulled a chair out with her foot.

"My name's Lacy Combs." She sat down careful, so as not to wake the boy. "My grandpa was Earl Combs, out in Quanah. Somebody shot him in his kitchen two weeks ago and took a cash box, and the constable's calling it a drifter robbery and waiting for it to blow past. I'm told your outfit takes claims when the local law won't."

"We do if there's somebody to claim against," Ross said. "A drifter nobody names is free. Who do you say it was?"

"I don't know. That's the problem." She rubbed the boy's back. "But I know my grandpa wasn't scared, and I know he wasn't fixing to hurt himself, and the whole county's decided he was one or the other on account of he sold his guns. They say it at the co-op like it's settled. It's not settled."

Ross looked at her a while. "The boy's yours?"

"Judd. He's four." Something crossed her face. "We were moving in with Grandpa the first of the month. I left my husband in March. Grandpa'd been after me all spring to come home."

Ross wrote the date down and underlined it.

****

Quanah, TX

Kelso's shop smelled like solvent and old carpet. He had Earl's collection racked along the back wall with paper tags on the trigger guards, waiting on the estate.

"Buy-book," Ross said, and Kelso hauled a green ledger onto the counter and turned it around. The Combs page ran two full sheets, every make and serial in Kelso's handwriting, with the total at the bottom: $19,400, paid by check.

"He dicker with you?"

"Earl? Dickered like a horse trader. Got me up from seventeen." Kelso laughed and quit laughing. "Wasn't a broke man's dicker, if that's your question. Broke men take the first number."

"Who else was in the shop when the collection came in?"

Kelso thought about it. "That was a Tuesday. Reyes come in for shells. The Miller kid put a deposit on a deer rifle." He tapped the counter. "And Merl Dodd stood right where you're standing while I entered the book. Asked me was that the whole Combs armory. I said down to the .22. He said, well, I'll be. Bought a box of primers and left."

Ross turned the ledger back around. "Anybody local been spending silver dimes?"

Kelso's eyebrows went up. "Now that you say it. Dodd's boy paid his feed tab at the co-op in dimes Saturday. Sue Ellen mentioned it because she had to roll them."

Out at the Combs place, Ross let himself in with Lacy's key. The kitchen had been cleaned by the church ladies. He went through the house slow, not looking for anything, letting the house say what it had to say. In the hall closet there was a paper sack from the hardware store. Inside were cabinet latches, the kind you put on low cupboards so a child can't get into the bleach. Two dozen outlet covers. A little gate meant for the top of a staircase, still in its wrapper. The receipt was dated the same day as the gun sale.

Ross stood in the hall a minute. "There you are," he said to nobody.

He called Mable from the phone at the co-op. "Everybody up here read it backwards," he said. "They see a man selling every gun he owns and they read fear. A scared man doesn't sell guns, he buys them. Nobody in the history of Texas ever got scared and disarmed."

"So why'd he sell?"

"Judd," Ross said. "Four years old, moving in the first of the month. Thirty-one guns in an old man's house, some of them loaded, and grandbaby fingers in everything. He wasn't emptying that house, Mable. He was making it safe. Sack of cupboard latches in the hall closet with the same date on the receipt."

Mable was quiet a second. "That's the sweetest thing I ever heard turned into evidence."

"It gets less sweet," Ross said. "One man stood at Kelso's counter and watched the whole collection go into the book, down to the .22. Same man's been feuding with Earl over water since two summers. And that man's boy just paid a feed bill in silver dimes."

****

The hearing was held in the Cattleman's Association back room, Judge Sikes presiding, retired but still keeping a docket for the panhandle counties. Dodd sat with the man from Panhandle Mutual, which carried him. Ross sat with Lacy. The chalkboard on the wall had two columns, CLAIM and ANSWER, and the stakes went up on it in Sikes's handwriting as they were posted.

Dodd's man laid their answer out first. "Merl Dodd had a water disagreement with the deceased, that's no secret and no crime. But nobody in his right mind walks into Earl Combs's house at night. Every soul in this county knew Earl kept better than thirty guns and had run off two prowlers in ten years. The house was a fort. Whoever went through that back door was a stranger who didn't know whose kitchen he was standing in. That right there is Merl's whole defense, and it's a good one."

"It is a good one," Ross said. "It's got one requirement in it, though. Merl had to believe the guns were still in the house."

He put the agency's money on the board. "Forty thousand dollars on one fact. On the ninth of last month, Merl Dodd stood at Kelso's counter and watched Earl's whole collection entered into the buy-book, and asked was that the armory entire, and was told down to the .22. Kelso will say so under bond. If Merl knew that house was empty of guns, his fort defense is done. If he wants to say he didn't know, let him put his money where mine is, and we'll hear Kelso out."

The Panhandle Mutual man leaned over and talked in Dodd's ear for a while. Dodd stared at the chalkboard and said nothing. Everybody in the room understood the arithmetic. Calling the stake meant putting Kelso under bond, and Kelso had no reason on earth to lie, and three other men had been in the shop that Tuesday.

They didn't call it.

After that the rest came out the way it does when the first stone's pulled. The dimes were entered. The co-op ledger was entered. A man from the vet clinic testified that Dodd had asked, two days before the killing, whether Earl's old dog was still boarded there over its hip surgery, and it was. Sikes gave his award before supper: the claim against Merl Dodd was made out, the whole of it, Earl and the burial and the estate's costs. It came to more than Dodd had or would ever have. Panhandle Mutual paid Lacy out that week, and then it opened its own ledger on Dodd, and a man Dodd's age would be working that number the rest of his life.

****

Lacy moved into the house the first of the month anyway. She said Earl would've been mad at her if she hadn't.

The first night, after Judd was down, she went through the hall closet for spare sheets and found the paper sack. She sat on the floor of the hall with the little latches in her lap and turned one over in her hand for a long time. Then she got a screwdriver from the kitchen drawer and put the first one on the cupboard under the sink, where the bleach was, the way he'd have wanted it done before the boy spent one night under that roof.

She did the outlet covers in Judd's room by lamplight. The stair gate she saved for morning.
