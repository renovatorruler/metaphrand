# FREE ROSS — THE POKER PLAYBOOK v1 (Texas Hold'em situations as dispute plays)

Category 3 of the research corpus. Every situation maps a real Hold'em concept onto the ARENA.md dispute mechanics (opening surety, metadata disclosure, fold/call/raise, surety caps, forced showdown, forfeit-with-seal, three outcomes, the public record). Format: NAME — the poker meaning — the dispute translation — what it's for dramatically.

## FAMILY 1 — BLUFFS

1. THE PURE BLUFF — Betting with nothing — staking big on a fact you know is false, priced so folding is cheaper than calling — the play that works until one opponent has showdown money and a reason.
2. THE SEMI-BLUFF — Betting with outs — staking on a fact you cannot prove yet but pending discovery might deliver (the toll-camera pull, the lab result) — drama of the card that is still coming.
3. THE OVER-BET — A stake far above the claim's normal price, polarized between nuts and air — forces the opponent to pay dearly just to find out which, and tells Ross more than it tells the market.
4. METADATA THEATER — The legal bluff surface: evidence described by shape only ("timestamped entrance footage," "sealed attestation") worded to imply more than it holds — the angle-shoot frontier where wording is the weapon.
5. THE BLOCKER BLUFF — Bluffing while holding the one card that makes the opponent's best hand impossible — staking false while holding sealed evidence that quietly kills their strongest counter-fact, so you know they cannot have what they claim.
6. THE STONE-COLD STORY — A bluff maintained across every betting round, a false fact staked consistently from filing to cap — the liar's marathon; the longer it runs, the more folding costs him.

## FAMILY 2 — TRAPS AND VALUE

7. THE SLOW-PLAY — Underbetting the nuts — posting a weak opening surety on a fact you hold clean video of, inviting raises to grow the forfeit pool before the reveal — greed with a mask of doubt.
8. THE CHECK-RAISE — Declining to stake, letting the opponent lead, then re-raising over him — procedural rope-a-dope; looks like weakness, is an ambush.
9. VALUE BETTING THIN — Small stakes on slightly-better-than-even facts, ground out over rounds — the grinder agency's style; no drama per hand, terrifying in aggregate.
10. EXTRACTING FROM THE NUTS — The hard part of an unbeatable hand is getting paid — an agency with irrefutable evidence must price stakes low enough that the guilty side keeps calling instead of folding early and cheap.
11. THE INDUCED BLUFF — Playing weak on purpose to make an honest-looking opponent try a steal — then calling it; manufacturing the other side's overreach.

## FAMILY 3 — READS AND HERO MOMENTS

12. THE TELL — The behavioral leak — a hand's throat-clear before a thin stake, an agency that always files on Thursdays when its evidence is weak — Ross's home ground, institutional and human.
13. THE HERO CALL — Calling a monster stake with weak evidence purely on a read that the other side is empty — Ross's signature play, and the one Enya calls gambling with clients' money.
14. THE BIG LAYDOWN — Folding a near-certain fact because the opponent's behavior says they hold the one thing that beats it — the discipline play; losing small as a form of genius.
15. TABLE IMAGE — The public record prices every player's history — the agency that never bluffs gets one free steal a decade; the maniac gets called forever and gets paid on its real hands.
16. THE LEVELING WAR — I know that he knows that I know — two elite hands staking against each other's psychology instead of the evidence, while the actual fact sits forgotten.
17. TILT — Staking emotionally after a loss — an agency or hand bleeding money to prove a point, and the sharks re-pricing every open case against them by nightfall.
18. THE SLOW ROLL — Delaying a certain-win reveal at showdown to savor it — costs nothing on paper, prices character, and makes enemies who wait years.

## FAMILY 4 — POSITION AND STRUCTURE

19. POSITION — The filing party stakes first, forever out of position — late position responds with information advantage, and procedural position is bought, traded, and fought over like a seat at the button.
20. THE BLINDS AND THE RAKE — Mandatory filing sureties fund the arbitrator pool — the ante that keeps frivolous claims out and the rake that makes arbitration a business with incentives worth an episode.
21. THE STRADDLE — Voluntarily doubling the opening stake blind to buy last action — an agency paying extra before seeing anything, to move last in every later round.
22. POT ODDS — Calling because the pool is already too big relative to the price — cold arithmetic that forces calls on doubtful facts; justice priced like a draw.
23. POT-COMMITTED — So much surety already sunk that folding now costs more than calling to the end — the trap of escalation; agencies riding a dying fact to showdown because retreat became the expensive option.
24. THE SHORT STACK — The poor claimant's whole strategy: one fact, maximum stake, immediately — no rounds to bleed through, forcing an early cheap showdown; poverty as tempo.
25. THE CHIP-LEADER BULLY — The rich agency raising every fact regardless of evidence, pricing the small side out of true claims — met in-world by contingency backers, claim assignment, and the caps.
26. THE CHECK-DOWN — Both sides stop staking and let the arbitrator rule on the thin record — neither wants to fund the pot; the anticlimax play, and sometimes the wisest.
27. THE CAP AND THE FORCED SHOWDOWN — Sureties cap at multiples of the claim, then either side may force the reveal — brinkmanship about WHEN to trigger it; the doomsday clock every big case carries.

## FAMILY 5 — MULTIWAY POTS

28. HEADS-UP VS MULTIWAY — Bilateral disputes play pure; add defendants and every stake signals to three tables at once — mass-harm cases are multiway pots where a fold by one changes everyone's odds.
29. THE SIDE POT — Some parties settle out mid-case while others play on — severed claims, partial peace, and awards divided by pool; the grandstand-collapse geometry.
30. THE SQUEEZE PLAY — A third claimant enters after two parties are heavily committed, leveraging their sunk sureties against both — the late-arriving claimant as predator.
31. COLLUSION — Two nominally opposed agencies soft-playing each other or squeezing a third — the cardinal market sin, detectable only in staking-pattern analytics on the public record.
32. THE FREEROLL — A position that can win but cannot lose — staking with an indemnity contract behind you, or playing a claim bought for pennies — and the market's hatred of players who risk nothing.

## FAMILY 6 — FATE CARDS

33. THE COOLER — Both sides hold genuinely strong, honestly believed evidence on contradictory facts — no villain, someone must still lose big; the tragedy hand.
34. THE BAD BEAT — The ninety-five-percent fact goes established-against at showdown — the recanting witness, the file that read differently in the open; the system's honest cruelty.
35. DRAWING DEAD — Raising rounds on a fact the opponent's sealed evidence already killed — playing on with no card in the deck that saves you, and everyone can see it but you.
36. THE MISREAD REVEAL — Evidence shown at showdown means something other than its holder believed — the file that proves too much, the alibi that convicts.
37. THE RIVERED RECORD — The reopened pot: a settled finding overturned by new evidence years later (recoverability canon) — the cold case as a river card that changes a finished hand.

## FAMILY 7 — THE META-GAME

38. FORFEIT-WITH-SEAL — Fold, pay in full, keep the evidence unexamined — the privacy valve as strategy: buying silence at a known price, and the read of WHAT a man protects by what he pays.
39. THE DEAD MAN'S HAND — A claim inherited or bought mid-play — heirs and claim-buyers play the same cards with different hearts; the widow folds where the specialist firm raises.
40. THE STOP-LOSS FOLD — Agency risk limits force folding a TRUE fact — truth priced out by portfolio math; the system's admitted error, and the client who has to hear it.
41. THE STAKING MARKET — Public disputes get spectator odds off the standing record — the town watching the line move on a neighbor's guilt; public opinion as a ticker.
42. THE HOUSE GAME — Arbitrator economics itself — the bench's incentive to be slow versus its reputation for being fast; whoever holds the rake shapes the game without touching a card.
