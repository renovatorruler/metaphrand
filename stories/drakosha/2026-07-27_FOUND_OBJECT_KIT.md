# «Фрося и Вася» — the found-object kit (precise prop and costume spec)

Purpose: exact, drawable descriptions of what these house spirits wear and carry, so no time is wasted guessing. Everything they own is an ordinary human household object at giant scale, or a scrap of the house's own cloth.

## THE THREE LAWS

1. RECOGNIZABLE FIRST. Every object must be instantly identifiable as its original human thing — the whole charm is the child watching saying "that's a THIMBLE." Never redesign an object into a fantasy version; a thimble stays a thimble, dents and all. Draw it with its real proportions, just enormous.
2. MAXIMUM TWO OR THREE VISIBLE PER CHARACTER. This is the important one. Two or three found objects reads as charming and resourceful. Five or more reads as a ragpicker or a junk heap — and that is the exact road back to the dirty-gremlin failure. Restraint IS the design.
3. CLEAN, NOT GRIMY. Everything is worn and mended, never soiled. Pale, light, soft debris (lint, feather, flour) reads as cozy. Dark smears, stains, and mud read as neglect. If in doubt, make it lighter.

## COSTUME — WHAT THE PATCHWORK IS MADE OF

The clothes are sewn from the house's leftover fabric, and each patch should be identifiable as a specific household textile: a floral pillowcase print, a striped dish towel, a child's dinosaur pyjama, a checked kitchen curtain, the ribbed cuff of a sock, a corduroy scrap, a bit of ticking from a mattress. Seams are whip-stitched in contrasting thread. Buttons never match: one four-hole white shirt button, one brass shank button, one wooden toggle.

THE FREE UPGRADE (costs nothing, pays off with the reading child): one patch on a child's clothing visibly MATCHES a fabric elsewhere in the house — the same floral as the curtain in the room they are standing in. Never remarked on. It says "we are made of this house" without a word.

Fit details that tell the story: Вася's trouser hems are rolled twice because they are hand-me-downs. Фрося's dress uses a handkerchief with the embroidered corner still showing at the hem. Everyone is barefoot, always; the only permitted footwear is a knitted sock with a hole at the toe, and it is funnier on one foot only.

### ВАСЯ (the becomer)
- THE LETTER POUCH (his signature, always present): a small stitched pouch of pale canvas on a shoelace strap, holding eight letter-chips — flat wooden tiles split from matchsticks or a clothespin, each with one letter inked on in black. When he is counting his letters he tips them into his palm.
- BELT: a brown shoelace tied at the waist, the plastic aglet dangling at his hip like a dagger.
- ONE OPTIONAL EXTRA (pick one per scene, never both): a thimble worn as a helmet, dented and silver; or a single dried pea carried in a pocket for no reason he can explain.

### ФРОСЯ (the Scribe)

- THE CHORE-LIST POUCH: a stitched pouch holding a rolled strip of paper — torn from the margin of a newspaper, one straight cut edge and one ragged tear edge, with box-and-tick marks in her handwriting.
- ONE OPTIONAL EXTRA: a large safety pin worn at the shoulder like a brooch, or a paperclip bent into a hook hanging at her hip.
- HAIR TIE, when she means business: a doubled brown rubber band, which loses to the hair by the end of every episode.

### МАМА-ДОМОВУШКА

- THE APRON: patchwork with two big pockets; wooden letter-chips in the left one.
- THE THIMBLE at her belt on a thread, used as a scoop and a measuring cup.

### МУСЯ (the little girl, ~1.5)
- SLEEPS IN A KNITTED MITTEN, tucked in to the thumb, her round cheek at the cuff. (A quiet salute to «Варежка», 1967 — the parents will feel it, the children will just find it cosy.)

## THE HOUSE-LINT LIBRARY (pick ONE per character, per scene — never more)

These say "lives behind the stove" while staying pretty rather than dirty. All are pale and soft.

- A DOWN FEATHER from a pillow: small, white, curved, with the barbs separating at the tip. The best of the lot — it reads as light, never as filth. Perfect caught in Фрося's hair.
- A DUST BUNNY: a soft grey-white fluff ball like a miniature tumbleweed, drawn with a fuzzy silhouette and no hard edges. It can roll past like a pet.
- A COBWEB STRAND: one fine grey thread trailing from a shoulder or an ear, with a single dew-bead on it catching the light.
- A FLECK OF FLOUR on a cheek or an eyebrow — kitchen days only, warm white.
- A CURL OF WOOD SHAVING caught in папа's hair: pale, spiral, translucent at the edge.
- A LOOSE THREAD caught on a sleeve, trailing a few centimetres. (This one doubles as РЫК's visual seed — when Вася's feelings go unspoken, that thread is what pulls.)

## HOUSEHOLD FURNITURE AND SCALE OBJECTS (the room, not the costume)

For the mandatory scale anchor in every illustration, drawn at their size: a wooden thread spool as a table or a tower; a matchbox as a bed; a thimble as a bucket or a stool; a shirt button as a dinner plate (the four holes clearly visible); a sewing needle carried like a spear, with the eye at the butt end; a walnut half-shell as a bowl or a boat; a cork as a stool; a Christmas-tree bulb as a hanging lamp; a bottle cap as a basin; a postage stamp as a wall picture; a domino as a bench; a spoon as a slide; a teacup as a bathtub.

Pick one or two per frame. The rule is the same as for costume: a few clear objects at true scale beat a cluttered dollhouse every time.

## NAME THINGS IN THE HOUSE'S OWN VOCABULARY (added 2026-08-19)

I described the chairs at the far wall as "kilim tub chairs". They are **sock chairs**. The author:

> They're sock chairs around the low tea table, but I do not know what you're describing.

"Tub chair" and "kilim" are furniture-catalogue words. Nothing in this house comes from a catalogue — every object is a found human thing repurposed, and its name should say what it was before. A sock chair is a sock: the foot makes the seat, the folded cuff makes the back and arms, and the patterned knit is the sock's own pattern. Calling it a tub chair describes the silhouette and throws away the joke.

**The rule: name an object by the human thing it used to be.** Not "a low round side table" but the **spool table**. Not "a small metal cup" but a **thimble cup**. Not "a woven basket bassinet" but the **tyre cradle**. Not "storage crates with bedding" but the **matchbox beds**. If a name for something has not been established yet, describe the human object plainly — "a sock", "a walnut shell" — and never reach for a design term to cover the gap.
