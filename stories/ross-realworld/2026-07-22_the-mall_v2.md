Frank Kessler sat across from Ross's desk and turned his wedding ring with his thumb. He was a big man gone soft, retired from the gas company, and he looked like he'd practiced this in the car.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "She's never lied to me once. Now she goes out every Tuesday and Thursday at two o'clock, she puts on lipstick to do it, and when I ask where she went she says the mall. Ellen hates the mall."

"Maybe she's buying you a present," Ross said.

"My birthday's in March." Frank stopped turning the ring. "I'm not a stupid man. I know what it is. I just want to know who."

They almost always said that. What they wanted was for Ross to tell them they were wrong, and most weeks he couldn't. He took the job anyway, two hundred a day and expenses, and said he'd have something by Friday.

On Tuesday he parked down the block from the Kesslers' place and watched Ellen come out at ten to two. She was sixty, and she'd dressed with care, a blue coat and a scarf, and she checked her face in the hall mirror before she shut the door. Frank had that part right. A woman doesn't fix her hair for the mall.

She drove a silver Camry twenty minutes across town and turned into a visitors' lot. Ross pulled in behind her and read the sign at the entrance. Meadowbrook. It was a hospice.

Ellen was inside an hour and forty minutes. When she came out she walked slower than she'd gone in. She got in the car and sat with her hands in her lap and didn't start it for a while, and when she finally pulled away Ross could see her face was wet.

Getting the name was easy, because a hospice tells you nothing but the sign-in book sits right there on the desk. Ellen Kessler, 2:05, to see a patient named Diane Povich. The volunteer at the desk went soft the way people do about someone near the end. Miss Povich didn't get many visitors, she said. Just the one lady, Tuesdays and Thursdays, since the spring.

The rest took an afternoon. Diane Povich and Ellen had grown up together, close as sisters, from the same block. Then twenty-two years ago they stopped cold. Nobody Ross talked to would say exactly what happened, only that Diane had said something about Frank that Ellen wouldn't stand for, and Ellen had chosen her husband and shut the door on her oldest friend and never opened it again. They'd lived eight miles apart for two decades and never said a word.

Then Diane got sick, the kind you don't come back from. She had no husband and no children, and a nephew in Arizona who sent a card. Somebody got word to Ellen. And Ellen started driving across town twice a week to sit with a woman she hadn't spoken to in twenty-two years and hold her hand while she died, and she told nobody, not even the man she'd been married to for thirty-one years.

He met Frank at a diner instead of the office. He told him straight. Where Ellen spent her afternoons, and who Diane Povich was.

Ross had done this enough to know the moment that usually comes, the man's shoulders dropping when he understands it isn't another man after all. It didn't come. Frank sat still and the blood came up in his face, and when he spoke his voice had gone flat and careful.

"You're sure it's Povich."

"I'm sure."

Frank looked at the table for a while. Then he took out his wallet and laid a twenty next to a coffee he hadn't touched.

"I'd rather it was a man," he said. He wasn't being cruel about it. He was telling Ross something true and it was costing him to say. "Right now, this afternoon, I'd rather she was in some motel with a fella from the gym. You can think whatever you want about that."

He put on his coat and left, and Ross sat there with two cold coffees and the feeling of having handed a man something worse than the thing he'd come in afraid of.

Ross knew the shape of it before he had any of the details, because the arithmetic is short. A man's wife slips out twice a week, and he would rather it were another man's bed than where she really goes. There's only one kind of thing on the far side of that. Whatever Diane Povich had said about Frank twenty-two years ago, and whatever Ellen had called her for saying it, Ellen had spent all the years since living as though her friend had lied. And you do not drive across town twice a week to sit at the deathbed of a woman you still believe lied to you.

Ellen had gone back to Diane because Ellen had decided, finally, that Diane told the truth.

Frank knew it the second Ross said the name. He would rather his wife were unfaithful this afternoon than have her believe, after thirty-one years, the true thing her oldest friend had told her about him and paid for with twenty-two years of silence. An affair he could have weathered. It happens to marriages and marriages go on. This was his wife quietly deciding who he had been the whole time, and there was nothing in it he could fight and no one he could hit.

Ross never learned what Frank had done. It wasn't in the job, and Frank had paid for the job and no more. But he thought a long time about the two cars he'd seen in that lot, the Camry and, one afternoon a week later, a pickup with a gas-company sticker nosed in beside it. He'd taken it for a good sign when he saw it. Now he wasn't sure. Frank hadn't driven out to Meadowbrook to sit with the dying.

He'd gone to see what his wife's face looked like when she came out.
