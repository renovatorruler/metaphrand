# THE FOUR OLDS — concept reboot v3

Status: chosen foundation for the engine-and-concept rebuild. This is a new
design, not a repair pass on the earlier screenplay.

## The decision

**Joss Reed, 27, is the protagonist.** He operates the communist
administration's camera drones, surface robots, and official Moon broadcast.
He once became known for Moon-hoax material and now holds a more corrosive
belief: every institution lies, every national story is advertising, and caring
only makes a person easier to fool.

The administration brings four veterans of America's return to the Moon as a
harmless **Legacy Witness Panel**. It wants old America visibly consenting to
its own surrender. The men accept because the government has accidentally
offered them transportation, equipment, and a worldwide audience.

The old men can physically stop the flag-removal ceremony. Only Joss can stop
the government from cutting the picture and rewriting what happened.

## The movie in one breath

A communist American president orders the six American Moon flags removed and
formally surrendered on July Fourth. To make the ceremony look voluntary, his
administration flies four forgotten lunar-program veterans to the Moon as
ceremonial witnesses and assigns a young, blackpilled Moon skeptic to control
the robots and the live broadcast. The old men quietly turn the government's
surrender pageant into an illegal Fourth of July cookout. After betraying them,
Joss must choose between the future the government has promised him and the
inheritance the old men have shown him through their conduct.

## Political and emotional contract

- The president is an actual communist. The film is openly anti-communist and
  openly sides with patriotic Americans who see removal of the flags as
  surrender and cultural erasure.
- The communist leadership is capable and dangerous. Its bureaucracy is absurd
  because it believes paperwork, euphemism, and control of the image can make
  ordinary people accept what they can plainly see.
- Supporters of the removal may appear and may remain furious at the ending.
  The movie does not split the difference or seek their approval.
- The emotional movement is anger, conspiracy, jeopardy, and joyful defiance.
  It does not become a funeral for the old men.
- The country does not become unanimous. The victory is that it becomes
  impossible to depict America as having surrendered.

## Setting and mission mechanics

- The story takes place in the late 2060s. Earl's generation worked on the
  Artemis-era return to the Moon and the first permanent lunar infrastructure.
  They inherited Apollo practices from the people who trained them; they are not
  implausibly young Apollo veterans.
- Earlier heritage crews stabilized the surviving flag material inside simple
  protective display frames. The administration is removing preserved American
  artifacts, not discovering six pristine flags still waving after a century.
- Five unmanned collectors deliver five framed flags to one sealed vault in
  lunar orbit. An automated lander carries that vault, Joss, and the four
  witnesses to a prepared site near the sixth flag. There is no unnamed local
  flight crew whose later choices rescue the plot.
- The ceremony includes a clear pressurized media pavilion and electric
  catering equipment so elderly witnesses can appear helmetless on camera. The
  pavilion, flags, and removal arm fit in the same wide image.
- Entering the removal arm's human-exclusion zone triggers a local safety
  interlock. Ground control cannot move the arm through a person without Joss's
  signed on-site authorization.
- The administration has advertised an independently verifiable live event.
  Emergency telemetry is therefore mirrored through public ground stations.
  Joss's local flight key can place raw suit and ceremony feeds on that channel;
  every command carries his signature and can be copied before Earth cuts it.
- The return vehicle and thirty-day habitat reserve remain available. The
  cookout is defiance with consequences, not a disguised group suicide.

## The antagonistic machine

The president is the face and author of the policy. He is not secretly
well-meaning and does not receive a sympathetic counter-speech. He understands
that destroying a national symbol is less useful than televising Americans
consenting to its destruction.

Pell, the lunar mission administrator, is the operational antagonist. He gives
Joss the conditional appointment, controls the remote commands, and keeps
trying to turn each emergency into a cleaner piece of television. He is not a
buffoon. His fatal mistake is believing that every human being is another
device whose permissions can be managed.

## Why the title now belongs to the story

The phrase **The Four Olds** begins inside the movie as hostile shorthand on the
administration's youth-facing coverage: four elderly lunar-program relics whom
it expects to control. It is used as an insult, not introduced through a
history lesson or made the formal name of a government program. An audience
needs no knowledge of Mao to understand the surface meaning: these are four old
men.

Beneath the surface, the quartet carries the historical Four Olds: old ideas,
old culture, old customs, and old habits. Each man has a dominant relationship
to one category, but none owns a clean symbolic box. Earl's preparation is also
habit; Dutch's standards are also culture; Stitch's cockpit culture contains
customs; Gunny's rituals express ideas. These labels are a private design
checksum. They are never supplied to a scene worker as character assignments,
spoken, taught in a seminar, printed on crates, or explained in dialogue.

By the ending, the title no longer means four dying men. It names four forms of
inheritance that have moved into younger hands.

## The five indispensable people

The cast contains five important people, but the dramatic center remains the
three-part design the project wanted:

- Joss is the protagonist who withdraws into cynicism.
- Earl is the cautious version of his choice: keep faith, protect life, and do
  not confuse courage with destruction.
- Gunny is the dangerous version: act now, break the machine, and accept the
  consequences.

Dutch and Stitch are not additional thematic protagonists. They make the
operation possible, create pressure inside the group, and force old and new
competence to depend on one another.

### JOSS REED, 27 — the successor

**Surface:** Joss is excellent with remote imaging, camera drones, surface
robots, and broadcast integration. He is calm with machines and evasive around
convictions. He does not talk in polished sarcasm or fake youth slang. His
shield is ordinary indifference: probably; who knows; it is all edited anyway.

**Submerged:** As a child, he wanted Apollo to be true. Later he watched trusted
institutions lie and decided that disbelief was safer than disappointment. His
Moon skepticism became a career before it became a settled conviction.

**Want:** Deliver a flawless removal broadcast and activate the signed permanent
lunar-operations appointment Pell has given him, effective only after a
successful ceremony.

**Contradiction:** He makes his living certifying the government's manufactured
reality while claiming that nobody can know what is real.

**Plot necessity:** Joss controls the removal robots and the official picture.
Without him, the old men can occupy the site, but the administration cuts the
feed, calls them senile extremists, and releases its own version. Joss makes the
truth public and gives the inheritance a future.

### EARL "CRICKET" DAWES, 79 — the commander

Earl was the backup commander for a canceled Artemis-era base rotation. He
trained for the Moon and never flew. He still treats a promise as binding after
the institution that made it has forgotten him.

**Surface:** Precise, quiet, and prepared. He answers emotional pressure with a
practical decision. He does not explain patriotism to Joss.

**Submerged:** He believes service should not require recognition, but part of
him desperately wants the Moon to prove that his lifetime of readiness was not
foolish.

**Contradiction:** He believes authority has limits, yet he spent fifty years
waiting for authority to call him.

**Plot necessity:** The administration needs Earl, its only recognizable
commander from the return-to-Moon generation, to legitimize the ceremony. Earl
agrees only if Dutch, Stitch, and Gunny come as the rest of his witness team.
Without Earl, nobody gets aboard. At the moment when the men can either reach
the flags first or rescue Joss, Earl orders the rescue. Without that decision,
the group fractures and Joss never sees the conduct that begins to change him.

### WALLACE "DUTCH" HAAS, 76 — the builder

Dutch is the life-support and surface-systems engineer whose early work still
sits under the modern lunar program.

**Surface:** He measures, labels, checks, and physically verifies anything a
screen claims is working.

**Submerged:** Early in his career, he once accepted somebody else's assurance
instead of checking a piece of hardware. The cause is never recounted; only the
habit remains.

**Contradiction:** He reveres standards and correct procedure while helping to
commit an enormous illegal act.

**Plot necessity:** Dutch owns cargo certification, life-support conversion, and
the machinery's physical bypass when the government locks the surface systems.
His analog knowledge alone is insufficient; Joss's modern control system alone
is insufficient. The plan works only when they stop treating the other's skill
as fraudulent.

### ELMORE "STITCH" McGREW, 74 — the pilot

Stitch was a test pilot and lunar-crew trainer. He carries the program's lived
culture: cockpit discipline, gallows humor, informal trust, and absolute
seriousness when another life depends on him.

**Surface:** Loose around danger, impatient around meetings, and funnier in what
he does than in anything he says.

**Submerged:** His hands have begun to betray him. He has told no one because he
would rather lose his last flight than be treated as fragile.

**Contradiction:** He looks reckless but will risk himself before gambling with
anyone else's life.

**Plot necessity:** The government remotely disables every autonomous crew
vehicle. Joss can unlock an old manual backup and provide a live drone map, but
only Stitch can pilot it safely across terrain missing from the automated route.
Neither man can move the group alone. When Stitch's hands fail, he must give part
of the flying to Joss rather than trying to remain the legend.

### LEO "GUNNY" TIBBS, 71 — the crew chief

Gunny was a Marine pad and EVA crew chief during the American return to the
Moon. He prepared celebrated men for history while remaining outside the
photographs.

**Surface:** He turns care into orders. He checks seals, feeds people, corrects
flag handling, and never explains why any of it matters.

**Submerged:** He says he never minded being omitted from history. Most people
do not believe him. He may not believe himself.

**Contradiction:** He is the most traditional man in the group and its greatest
improviser. He follows customs precisely and laws selectively.

**Plot necessity:** Gunny owns the EVA rescue, the flag-site rigging, and the
non-destructive restraint that stops the extraction arm without damaging the
flag. He also transforms the government's ceremonial equipment into a shared
table. His answer to the standoff is not a weapon or a speech. It is to feed
allies, opponents, and anyone willing to sit down without surrendering the flag.

## What actually passes to Joss

The men do not give Joss four lessons. They give him work, responsibility, and
reasons to be angry with them. The transfer also changes the old men:

- Earl trusts Joss with a real life, then learns that honorable action can still
  be erased when nobody controls the record.
- Dutch makes Joss check the physical truth, then accepts that transparent
  software can be workmanship rather than deception.
- Stitch treats Joss as capable, then has to place real control in his hands.
- Gunny shows that custom survives through participation, then allows Joss to
  change the practice while preserving its purpose.

At the climax, Joss makes one visible choice: he uses his access to the network
and machinery the state controls to keep the real event public. No character or
scene itemizes what he inherited. He does not imitate the old men; he contributes
the modern capability without which their stand would disappear.

## Story spine

### Act I — Old America is invited to its own surrender

1. The film opens with the communist president announcing that all six preserved
   American flags will be removed from the Moon. Five will be collected
   robotically. On July Fourth, the last flag will be lowered live and all six
   formally surrendered to a communist-led international authority.
2. Joss is introduced operating the retrieval robots. His old Moon-hoax material
   makes him useful to the administration: after inspecting a heritage site in
   person, he is expected to recant live and certify the surrender broadcast as
   the first lunar event his younger audience should trust. Pell gives him a
   signed permanent lunar-operations appointment that activates after a
   successful ceremony.
3. The administration recruits Earl for its Legacy Witness Panel. It wants a
   forgotten commander from America's return-to-Moon generation standing behind
   the ceremony so coercion looks like consent. Earl accepts only if Dutch,
   Stitch, and Gunny join him as his team, and the administration agrees because
   four old faces improve the image.
4. The four accept publicly and are denounced as sellouts by their own side. The
   administration believes age, money, and gratitude will keep them obedient.
5. The audience sees their real response. They lay out the equipment from their
   decades-old Fourth of July cookout and begin fitting it inside the ceremony's
   approved cargo. The cookout is a promise to the audience, not a clever twist.
6. Training and rehearsal bring Joss into conflict with them. He treats them as
   manageable historical content. They keep ignoring their marks to check the
   actual equipment, revise unsafe procedures, and prepare for the Moon.
7. Joss spots suspicious cargo changes and files an equipment irregularity, but
   he does not yet know the plan. The administration clears it: the surrender
   image needs authentic old Americans, every surface machine is remotely
   controlled, and officials are certain four old passengers cannot defeat the
   state.
8. They launch under watch. The trap now holds everyone: the government needs
   the old men for legitimacy; the men need Joss's systems; Joss needs the
   mission for his future.

### Act II — Proof is not inheritance

1. An early equipment failure forces Dutch and Joss to solve one problem
   together. Stitch trusts Joss's live route correction, and the group earns one
   small success before anybody changes sides.
2. Joss finds real Apollo remains and accepts that the landing occurred. It does
   not convert him. To him, this is merely one fewer lie in a world made of lies.
3. The administration orders him to suppress raw footage that contradicts its
   approved history. He complies and watches his skepticism become material for
   a new official lie.
4. The old men make their first move toward the vault holding the five recovered
   flags. Joss catches them and knowingly turns them over. They are confined, and
   Joss is praised publicly for protecting the mission. This is his first real
   betrayal, not a temporary misunderstanding.
5. The men manually bypass their holding hatch, take the maintenance rover, and
   head for the final flag. Joss pursues alone under Pell's order. A remote
   override sends his rover across an unsafe route, and he crashes outside his
   walk-back oxygen range.
6. The four can continue to the flag or turn back for the person who betrayed
   them. Earl turns them back, Stitch gets Gunny across the unsafe terrain, and
   Gunny performs the EVA recovery. The delay gets all four recaptured. Nobody
   records the rescue, gives a speech, or asks Joss to change.
7. The administration publicly describes the accident in a way Joss knows is
   false. He finally sees that his role is not to verify reality but to lend his
   reputation to whatever image the state needs.
8. Joss refuses to lock them up again. He releases them and opens the
   recovered-flag vault. He still tells himself that he is documenting the story
   rather than joining it.
9. The ceremony begins in seventy-four minutes. Their portable oxygen supports
   only fifty-five minutes on the safe route. Joss can unlock an old manual rover
   and map the shorter crossing; Stitch must pilot it across terrain automation
   has rejected. Remote locks, an injured old body, and their mutual distrust
   make modern skill and inherited practice equally necessary.
10. Gunny rejects a destructive escalation and returns them to the cookout plan
   they packed on Earth. He begins laying out the ceremony equipment while the
   others prepare the non-destructive hold.

### Act III — The image changes hands

1. The live surrender ceremony begins. The president speaks from Earth while
   the automated removal arm moves toward the final American flag.
2. The four men enter the removal arm's exclusion zone and stop the machinery
   without weapons. They place the five recovered flags with the sixth and
   occupy the site. The local safety interlock holds unless Joss personally
   authorizes the arm to move through them.
3. The administration orders Joss to authorize the arm, cut the feed, and
   preserve his legal defense by claiming the old men forced him.
4. The men tell him to take that option. Their tradition is not a loyalty test,
   and they will not purchase his agreement with their rescue.
5. Joss refuses the authorization. Using his signed local flight key, he removes
   the broadcast delay, patches the ceremony and suit cameras into the mirrored
   emergency channel, and releases its decode key. The public record identifies
   him as the author, destroying his coercion defense, permanent appointment,
   and freedom. Independent stations copy the feed before Earth can suppress it.
6. Gunny and Joss convert the government's solemn ceremony into the most illegal
   Fourth of July cookout in history. The expensive reconciliation chairs become
   lawn chairs. A clear pressure pavilion intended for the official reception
   becomes the dining room, so people can actually remove helmets and eat. The
   ceremonial food equipment becomes the grill. The official lights illuminate
   the six flags outside and the shared table within.
7. Joss is the first person from the removal side to cross into the cookout. He
   naturally takes the griddle when Gunny's hands need rest while Earl, Dutch,
   Stitch, and Gunny remain occupied with the flags, machinery, feed, and table.
8. Young lunar flight controllers on Earth refuse orders to shut down the
   habitat and launch an enforcement vehicle. Outside their building and across
   America, younger people pull grills into streets, parking lots, farms,
   rooftops, and ball fields. Others condemn them. The film stays with the people
   acting.
9. The movie ends at the Moon cookout. The president remains dangerous and the
   consequences remain real, but his required image of American surrender is
   gone. The final image shows old and young maintaining the cookout together,
   with Joss working the grill and keeping the feed alive.

No final line is locked at concept stage. The ending must work as a silent image
before any dialogue is considered.

## Comic engine

The central comic collision is simple:

**The government needs four uncontrollable old Americans as obedient historical
props, and the men keep turning every piece of the surrender ceremony into part
of their cookout.**

The comedy comes from goals and visible behavior:

- Joss tries to stage solemn rehearsal footage while the men leave their marks
  to inspect unsafe machinery or move a chair where it is actually useful.
- The government gives therapeutic names to plainly coercive acts; the camera
  shows the ordinary fact underneath.
- The old men are physically limited, stubborn, petty, frightened, and capable.
  Their dignity is protected; their vanity and habits are available for comedy.
- The state has a procedure for every expected protest and no procedure for its
  own ceremonial witnesses calmly beginning lunch.
- Modern automation and old practice each fail separately and succeed together.

No joke is approved because a model can explain its setup and payoff. Lines and
comic beats remain proposals until the author actually finds them funny.

## Engine acceptance tests

The story engine must reject or stop this project when any of these conditions
is true:

1. **Audience allegiance fails:** the film starts treating surrender as a
   respectable position it must validate or balance.
2. **Joss is removable:** deleting him leaves the climax, public victory, or
   generational meaning intact.
3. **Any old man is decorative:** deleting one changes only dialogue rather than
   breaking a necessary decision, skill, or action.
4. **Conversion becomes a lesson:** Joss changes because somebody explains
   America, Mao, patriotism, or the Four Olds to him.
5. **Physical proof performs the whole arc:** discovering Apollo hardware makes
   Joss instantly patriotic.
6. **The categories surface:** ideas, culture, customs, and habits are assigned
   aloud, printed on props, or arranged as four classroom examples.
7. **Old defeats young:** the story proves only that elderly competence is better
   than modern competence instead of making both generations necessary.
8. **The future remains abstract:** younger people do not make costly choices or
   physically continue the cookout.
9. **Comedy requires explanation:** a joke depends on historical knowledge,
   hidden structure, or an editor explaining why it is clever.
10. **The state self-certifies taste:** an LLM judge labels a title, joke, or
    emotional reaction successful without human approval.

## Discarded from the earlier version

- No lecture or seminar explaining Mao's Four Olds.
- No category names stenciled on crates.
- No "We came in the mail."
- No billionaire mastermind secretly arranging the victory.
- No false political balance.
- No old-men-only victory that dies with them.
- No conversion speech.
- No cookout hidden merely to manufacture a clever reveal.

## Physical-reality note

The screenplay must never show six pristine original flags. NASA's historical
record says the Apollo 11 flag was probably knocked over during ascent, while
orbital images suggest that flags at some later landing sites remained upright.
That is why this version uses surviving material stabilized by later heritage
crews inside protective frames. The political act is the removal of preserved
American artifacts, not the implausible collection of untouched cloth.
