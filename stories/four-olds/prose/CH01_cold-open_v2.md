# MOON KING — Chapter 1, v2 (the cold open; five jumps, one room)

On election night, Buck kept the diner open late and turned the sound up. The nine men who ate breakfast at Roy's every morning of their lives sat in the booths with pie and coffee and watched the numbers come in. Marwani carried forty states. Nobody said much of anything. Around midnight Earl paid for his pie, and Buck wiped down the counter and flipped the sign, and the parking lot emptied one truck at a time into the dark.

On the twentieth of January it snowed in Dawes County, and the television over the pie case showed the Capitol steps. The new president took his hand off the Bible, turned to the cameras, and said the part that would run all year. To the nations of the world, said President Marwani, and to every people we have wronged, on behalf of two hundred and fifty years of Americans, living and dead, we are sorry. The crowd on the steps applauded for a long time, and some of them were crying. At the counter, Earl salted his eggs.

In April the television showed a gray cargo jet on a runway in a foreign capital, and fork trucks running pallets of shrink-wrapped money down the ramp. A man from the State Department stood in front of the airplane and called it a good start.

Like sod, Junior said. They stack it just like sod.

In June the hearings started. The screen showed an old man sitting alone at a witness table behind a folded card that said HALE, while a senator half his age read him a question that took a full minute to get to its point. The sound was down, but the crawl along the bottom of the screen said the administration meant to bring Frontier Space, the private company that flew the country's rockets and landed them standing up, under federal control. They had set a photograph of a Frontier booster on an easel behind the senators, and the old man at the table looked at the senator the way a man looks at rain when the hay is already cut.

Nine days before the Fourth of July, the radio on the grill shelf read out an announcement from the county administrator's office, tucked between the hog report and the pool hours, in the fast flat voice of a young man reading side effects. The county fireworks display was canceled this year in observance of national air quality goals. Personal fireworks were now grouped with open burning under the new framework. Residents still holding fireworks could bring them to the amnesty barrels at the fairgrounds through Friday, no questions asked. The Fourth itself remained a federal holiday, and families were encouraged to gather quietly.

The radio moved on to the pool hours.

Nobody at the counter said anything for a while. Thirty red, white, and blue paper flags on toothpicks stood in a foil-wrapped coffee can by the register, left over from Memorial Day. A man from the co-op took one on his way out and worked it into the band of his hat, and his truck started on the second try.

Amnesty barrels, Earl said, to his eggs.

No questions asked, Junior said. That's big of them.

Buck stood at the coffee urn with the pot full in his hand and looked at the radio for a while.

Huh, Buck said.

Then he came down the counter and filled every cup on it without being asked.
