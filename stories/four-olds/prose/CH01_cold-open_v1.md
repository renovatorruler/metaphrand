# MOON KING — Chapter 1 (the cold open; novel prose, per SCENEMAP v14 scene v01)

POSITION: page one. The world before the heroes. One location. The four olds do not appear; they are not yet needed. Rage rungs one and two only.

---

The television over the pie case at Roy's had been on the same channel for twenty years, mostly because the remote was lost and the set was bolted too high for anybody to reach without the good ladder, and the good ladder was at Buck's house. Six mornings a week it played to the same nine men, and the nine men played back, and that was breakfast in Dawes County.

This morning the television was showing the inauguration again. Four months old and they were still running it, the way they used to run the moon landing on anniversaries. The new president stood on the Capitol steps in the cold with his hand still warm from the Bible and said the part everyone knew by heart now.

To the nations of the world, said President Marwani, and to every people we have wronged, on behalf of two hundred and fifty years of Americans, living and dead. We are sorry.

The crowd on the television applauded for a long time. Some of them were crying.

At the counter, Earl salted his eggs.

You seen where they sent the money, said the man next to Earl, whose name was Junior and who was sixty-eight. It was on last night. Airplane full of money on wood pallets, like sod. Fork trucks driving it around.

I seen it, Earl said.

They had a fella there to explain it, Junior said. He called it a good start.

Buck came down the counter with the pot and filled Earl, then Junior, then the two hats from the co-op, without anybody asking and without missing a cup. Behind him on the grill shelf the radio was doing the local news at seven, which was a hog report, a road closure, and then the county administrator's office, a young voice reading fast, the way they read the side effects at the end of pill commercials.

The county fireworks display, said the radio, was canceled this year in observance of national air quality goals. Personal fireworks were now grouped with open burning under the new framework. Residents holding fireworks could bring them to the amnesty barrels at the fairgrounds through Friday, no questions asked. The Fourth of July itself, the radio said, remained a federal holiday, and families were encouraged to gather quietly.

The radio moved on to the pool hours.

Nobody at the counter said anything for a while. On the register by the door stood a coffee can wrapped in foil, stuck full of little paper flags on toothpicks, left over from Memorial Day. Red, white, and blue, thirty of them. A man from the co-op took a toothpick flag on his way out and worked it into the band of his hat, and pushed out into the June sun, and his truck started on the second try.

Amnesty barrels, Earl said, to his eggs.

No questions asked, Junior said. That's big of 'em.

Up on the television, the inauguration had given way to a Senate hearing. An old man sat alone at the witness table with his name on a card in front of him, HALE, and a senator half his age was reading him a question that filled the whole screen with small words. The sound was down. The old man at the table looked at the senator the way a man looks at rain when the hay's already cut.

Buck stood under the television with the pot in his hand and watched the president say sorry one more time, over the senator's shoulder, in the little box in the corner where the news kept it going all day.

Huh, Buck said.

Then he went and got the ketchup for table four, because Earl's grandson liked ketchup on his hash browns, and it was almost seven-thirty, and the Fourth was nine days out.
