# MOON KING — Chapter 1, v5

The election was called a few minutes past eleven, from a network studio in New York where the map behind the anchor desk had been filling in blue since Ohio. The anchor touched his ear, looked once at the map, and told the country that Marwani had carried forty states. The feed went to the Marwani headquarters in Chicago, where the balloons were already down and a crowd too young to have voted twice chanted one word, Unity, over and over.

***

On inauguration morning snow fell on the Capitol steps, fine and dry, and the new president took the oath with his breath showing. When the chief justice was done with him, President Marwani turned to the cameras. To the nations of the world, he said, and to every people we have wronged, on behalf of two hundred and fifty years of Americans, living and dead, we are sorry. On the Mall they applauded for a long time. Some of the people in the front rows were crying.

***

The cargo jet on the runway outside Tehran sat with its ramp down and its engines off, and the air over the tarmac was already shaking with heat at nine in the morning. Fork trucks ran pallets of hundred-dollar bills out of its belly, shrink-wrapped in bricks and stacked the way sod comes from the farm. A man from the State Department stood at a folding lectern beside the nose gear and called it a good start.

***

In the Senate hearing room the witness table had one chair at it, and the folded card on the table said HALE. Somebody had stood a photograph of a Frontier booster on an easel beside the dais, the kind that lands standing up. A senator half the witness's age read him a question that ran three sheets of paper, and the end of it asked whether the American road to space ought to rest in the hands of one private corporation, the Frontier Space Corporation, or in the hands of the American people themselves. Hale pulled the microphone to him.

Whose hands are those, Senator, he said.

The senator looked down and began squaring his papers on the desk.

***

Roy's was the diner at the highway end of Main Street in Dawes County, and the same nine men ate breakfast there six mornings a week. Earl and Junior had the two stools nearest the register. The morning Buck put the window air conditioner in over the door, the radio on the grill shelf read out an announcement from the county administrator's office, tucked between the hog report and the pool hours, in the fast flat voice of a young man reading side effects. The county fireworks display was canceled this year in observance of national air quality goals. Personal fireworks were now grouped with open burning under the new framework. Residents still holding fireworks could bring them to the amnesty barrels at the fairgrounds through Friday, no questions asked. The Fourth itself remained a federal holiday, and families were encouraged to gather quietly.

The radio moved on to the pool hours.

For a while the only sound in the place was the griddle. Thirty red, white, and blue paper flags on toothpicks stood in a foil-wrapped coffee can by the register, left over from Memorial Day. A man from the co-op took one on his way out and worked it into the band of his hat, and his truck started on the second try.

Amnesty barrels, Earl said, to his eggs.

No questions asked, Junior said. That's big of them.

Nine days, Earl said.

Buck stood at the coffee urn with the pot full in his hand and looked at the radio for a while.

Huh, Buck said.

Then he came down the counter and filled every cup on it without being asked.
