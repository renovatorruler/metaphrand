# MOON KING — Chapter 1, v8

Forty seconds before the top of the hour, the man from the decision desk took off his headset and crossed the studio floor to the anchor desk, and instead of using the talkback he bent down and said it into the anchor's ear. On the monitor wall behind them, the map of the country had been filling in blue since Ohio went early. The anchor looked up at him. The man nodded and walked back the way he had come. A camera swung around to center, the floor manager held up five fingers and folded them one at a time, and the red light came on.

"It's eleven o'clock in the East," the anchor said, "and Consolidated News is now ready to project that Governor Marwani of Illinois has been elected president of the United States. The map behind me is showing forty states."

In the control room upstairs somebody opened a bottle they had been keeping under the switcher. Down on the studio floor a grip watched the monitor with his arms crossed for a moment, and then went back to coiling his cable.

***

On the Capitol steps the cold made everyone's breath show, so that the crowd on the Mall looked like it was steaming. Marwani took the oath bare-headed, his hand on his family's Bible, repeating the words after the chief justice in a slow clear voice, and when he had said the last of them he did not shake anyone's hand. He turned to the bank of cameras and took hold of both sides of the lectern.

"Before my inaugural address, there is one thing that must be said, and it is not addressed to you," he said. "It is addressed to the world. To the nations of the world, and to every people we have wronged, on behalf of two hundred and fifty years of Americans, living and dead: we are sorry."

For a moment the Mall stayed quiet. Then the applause started near the stage and rolled back over the reflecting pool, and it went on so long that the chief justice sat down in his chair to wait it out. In the front rows people were crying and holding up their phones.

***

At the airport outside Tehran the press pool stood inside a taped square on the tarmac, and the heat came up through their shoes. Behind the tape, a gray American cargo jet sat with its ramp down, and forklifts came out of its belly one after another, carrying pallets of hundred-dollar bills shrink-wrapped in plastic and stacked the way sod is stacked. A man from the State Department stood at a folding lectern with his jacket over his arm.

"Sir, is there a total?" one of the reporters said. "A number?"

"There is a number," the man said. "What I would emphasize today is that this is a good start."

A forklift went by behind him with its backing alarm going, and he waited for it, and the pallet on its forks dragged a shadow across the lectern.

***

The Senate hearing room had marble to shoulder height and cameras in every corner, and the witness table had one chair at it. The folded card in front of the chair said MR. HALE. Beside the dais somebody had stood a photograph of a Frontier booster on an easel, the kind that lands on its tail, taken at the moment of touchdown with its legs out.

The senator from Connecticut read his question off three sheets of paper. While he read, Hale poured half a glass of water, drank it, and set the glass down.

"...and given that you, Administrator, have yourself led NASA for eleven years, under three presidents of both parties," the senator read, turning to his last sheet, "and given the sacred character of the national patrimony, which is not for sale and never was, I ask you, sir, and I ask it in the spirit of unity: should the American road to space rest in the hands of a single private corporation, the Frontier Space Corporation, or in the hands of the American people themselves?"

Hale pulled the microphone to him by its neck.

"Whose hands are those, Senator?" he said.

Up in the gallery one person laughed once. The senator looked down and began squaring his three sheets against the desk, and the chairman asked the gallery to come to order.

***

Roy's was the diner at the highway end of Main Street in Dawes County, and the same nine men ate breakfast there six mornings a week. The morning the heat arrived, Buck spent the first hour of daylight on a stepladder, putting the window air conditioner in over the door, and the breakfast crowd came in under his elbow. Earl and Junior took the two stools nearest the register. Buck came down the ladder, washed his hands, and got the second pot going, and the radio on the grill shelf finished the hog report and went over to a recorded announcement from the county administrator's office, a young man reading fast, the way the side effects get read at the end of a pill commercial.

"This year's county fireworks display is canceled in observance of national air quality goals," the voice said. "Personal fireworks are now classified with open burning under the updated framework. Residents holding fireworks may surrender them at the amnesty barrels at the county fairgrounds through Friday, no questions asked. The Fourth of July remains a federal holiday. Families are encouraged to gather quietly."

The radio went on to the pool hours. For a while the only sound in the place was the griddle.

"Amnesty barrels," Earl said, to his eggs.

"No questions asked," Junior said. "That's big of them."

"Nine days out," Earl said.

Buck came down the counter with the fresh pot and filled every cup on it, and nobody had to ask.

On his way out, one of the co-op men stopped at the register, where thirty red, white, and blue paper flags on toothpicks stood in a foil-wrapped coffee can, left over from Memorial Day. He took one and worked it into the band of his hat, and pushed out into the sun, and his truck started on the second try.
