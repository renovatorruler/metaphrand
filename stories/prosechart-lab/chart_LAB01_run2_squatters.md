The lawyer told me twice on the phone that the property was vacant. I drove down with a roll of contractor bags and a tape gun and the radio off.

There was a bike in the yard. Kid's bike, purple, training wheels.

I stood by my truck a while. (You learn this working a rush. Don't move till you know what's on fire.) Curtains in the front window. New curtains, yellow. My mother never hung yellow a day in her life.

I knocked on my own father's door.

She opened it on the chain. Maybe forty. Work shirt from the Valero on 73. Behind her a TV going and a kid asking who is it, and a smell that stopped me cold, and it took a second to place it because I hadn't smelled it in thirty years: Community Coffee, the red bag, brewed too strong. His brand. His way.

I said who I was. Took her a moment. Then she undid the chain and stood square in the door and said, so you're Ray. He kept your picture on the icebox.

Didn't know what to do with that. Still don't.

The house goes to auction the twelfth, I said. I got to have it cleared. I'm sorry about your situation but I got papers.

She said, I got papers too, and went and got them. A spiral notebook. Every month for two years, in his hand: May, one-fifty, paid. June, one-fifty, paid. Little checkmarks like he did on his shift sheets at the refinery. No lease. No receipts. Just an old man's ledger and her name at the top of it, Darla, and under it, in his writing, a phone number and the words call if trouble.

That's not a lease, I said.

No, she said. It ain't. She didn't argue it. That was worse. She stood there with her notebook that wasn't anything, in the door of a house that wasn't hers, and mine on a paper I never wanted, and neither of us was wrong and one of us had a bike in the yard.

The kid came to the door then. Little girl, front teeth out. She said, are you Mr. Buck's boy?

Nobody called him Buck. His name was Wallace. My mother called him Wally and the refinery called him Whitfield and I called him sir until the day I quit calling him.

Yes ma'am, I said. I guess I am.

She said Mr. Buck fixed her bike chain and showed her a bird on the wire, a scissor-tail. She said he told her it flies all the way from Mexico every year to sit on that same damn wire, and I said, he said damn? and she said, yeah, and looked proud about it, and Darla said, go on inside.

We stood there. The two of us and the door he'd painted and the yard he'd mowed and the whole thing that wasn't either of ours, not really, his, and him gone, and the twelfth coming whether anybody on that porch deserved it or not.

I'm not a hard-luck story, she said. I clean at the plant nights. I pay. I'll pay you.

It's not mine to rent, I said. It's sold or it's nothing. That's how he set it up. Talk to the bank.

She looked at me the way you look at a wrong ticket. Not mad at the cook. Mad at the order.

I'll be back Saturday, I said. For his things. Just his things, for now.

Went back to the truck. Sat there. On the wire over the yard there was a scissor-tail, tail streaming out behind it like something escaping a pot, and I sat in a truck full of contractor bags outside a house full of somebody else's supper, his coffee in a stranger's kitchen, his nickname in a kid's mouth, more of him alive in there than anywhere I had a paper to.

Two years she knew him. I hadn't called in three.

Saturday, then. Bring boxes. Figure out which one of us is the trespasser.
