The house was smaller than I remembered. They always are, people say, but knowing it would be smaller had not prepared me for how much smaller — as if the years had been quietly subtracting from it, room by room, while I was away living my larger life.

I stood on the porch with the key the lawyer had mailed me and watched the late afternoon light slant through the pecan trees, gilding the dust that hung in the air. The whole street had that abandoned-museum quality now: houses like exhibits of a vanished way of living, lawns gone to seed, a tricycle rusting in a yard where no child had played in years. Somewhere down the block, a screen door banged — once, twice — like the town clearing its throat to say something it never quite managed to say.

The key turned easily. That surprised me. I had expected resistance, some small mechanical protest, the house taking my father's side. Instead the door swung open onto the smell of him — pipe tobacco and machine oil and the faint sweetness of the peppermints he kept in every drawer — and I had to stand there a moment, one hand on the frame, letting it move through me.

Inside, everything waited exactly where he had left it. His reading glasses sat folded on the arm of the recliner, still holding the shape of his attention. The TV Guide from the week he died lay open to a Wednesday. In the kitchen, a single plate, a single cup, a single fork in the drying rack — the quiet arithmetic of a man who had learned to live alone and never learned to like it.

I had come to empty the house. That was the phrase the lawyer used, and it had seemed like a reasonable phrase in his office, with its implication of boxes and weekends and a dumpster in the driveway. Standing in the living room, I understood that the phrase was a lie. You do not empty a house. The house empties you — drawer by drawer, photograph by photograph, until you are sitting on the floor of your childhood bedroom at midnight, holding a baseball glove gone stiff with age, unable to remember the last time you called him.

The auction was in nine days. The realtor had been clear about the timeline, apologetic but clear: the buyer wanted the lot, not the house. They would tear it down within the month — the porch, the pecan trees, the kitchen where my father had taught me to make coffee too strong and drink it anyway. Progress, the realtor called it. The town needed the tax base. I understood, the way you understand weather: it wasn't personal, and it was coming regardless.

I started, the way you start anything impossible, with the easiest room. The bathroom surrendered its history without argument — razors, aspirin, a comb still carrying a few silver hairs I couldn't bring myself to throw away and couldn't justify keeping. By dusk I had filled six boxes and opened none of the ones that mattered.

That night I slept in my old room, under a ceiling whose water stains I still knew by heart, and listened to the house settle around me — the ticks and sighs of old wood cooling, a language I had forgotten I spoke. Tomorrow there would be the garage, the attic, the desk with its locked bottom drawer. Tomorrow the real excavation would begin.

But tonight the house and I just lay there together in the dark, two things that had belonged to him, neither of us quite ready to belong to anyone else.
