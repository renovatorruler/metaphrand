# «ज़ुबान» (Zubaan) — modern Tejaji, v1 design (STASHED 2026-07-26)

Standalone contemporary story. NOT part of AMAL (author explicitly severed it). Setting locked by the author: contemporary India, Bangalore, protagonist a North Indian working in tech.

## The retelling principle (why this and not the first attempt)
Tejaji's moral physics, stripped of props: a man commits an act of KINDNESS and thereby incurs a FATAL DEBT to a creditor who is entirely in the right and completely unable to collect; he asks for time to serve a third party and is released on his word alone; the errand ruins him; he returns anyway; the payment nearly fails because nothing clean is left of him; he pays with the organ he made the promise with; he becomes the protector against the thing that killed him.

REJECTED first attempt (the honest-witness version): it made the creditor a villain. The moment the ones taking his life are gangsters, a moral riddle becomes a moral cartoon and the hero is obviously correct — the mode-safe story. STANDING TEST for any retelling: is the one who takes his life RIGHT to take it?

## The title carries the design
ज़ुबान = the tongue in the mouth + the language you speak + your given word (ज़ुबान देना). All three are the same object in this story.

## The story
He is from a small northern city, eight years in Bangalore, mid-tier product company, the built accent, the English that opens counters, the money sent home, the family success nobody back home can quite describe.

Driving back late he comes on a crowd on a service road — a man being beaten over a rumour and a phone video, the usual arithmetic of a stranger who looked wrong in the wrong lane. He would have driven past; everyone drives past. What stops him is that the man on the ground is crying out in HINDI and he understands it. (The fire; and the tongue's first appearance — he intervenes because of a language.) He gets him out using his shirt, his English, his phone held up like a weapon, the class markers that make a crowd hesitate. He does everything right.

The man does not die — the engine of the whole story. He lives wrecked (spine or head), and would have been gone in twenty minutes if nobody had stopped. The family comes down from the north — wife, brother, a child. After weeks in the government hospital the wife says it, not angrily, not shouting: he was going; you brought him back; so he's yours also now. She is RIGHT. No court, no paper, no enforcement — one sentence in a corridor.

He gives his word. Then, as Teja does, he asks for time first, and the time is for somebody else: his sister's wedding is eight months out and he is paying for it. They agree because agreeing is all they can do.

The eight months destroy him, and the setting earns its place here: two households on one salary, so he takes work he isn't allowed to take. The layoffs arrive — impersonal, quarterly, nobody's fault, a call from a stranger in another city. The moonlighting surfaces at exit. Terminated for cause, which in that industry is quiet and permanent: the background check, the reference, the phrase that follows you. He does the wedding, smiles in the photographs, then goes back to the family with nothing.

The "no clean place to bite" scene plays exactly as in the original: they look at him — what are you now? No salary, no company, no standing. Nothing left of you to take.

So he offers the last thing that still works, the same organ: I'll speak. And he does, for years — the disability certificate, the compensation scheme nobody at the counter mentions, the FIR never registered, the insurance clerk who finds the file when the request arrives in fluent English, the school admission, the hospital that tells people to come back Thursday who cannot afford Thursday. He is their mouth. The one asset he built for himself he spends entirely on a family he met once, on the worst night of their lives.

He becomes known for it; other families send for him, the way people once carried a snakebite to a Tejaji shrine, and for the same reason — he is protection against the exact machinery that ruined him, the one that eats anyone who cannot speak its language. He gets nothing. He does not marry. He is not warm about it; he is tired and often rude, and he keeps going.

The injured man dies eventually. The debt is discharged and he does not stop, because by then it is not a debt, it is what he is.

LAST IMAGE: he goes home north for the first time in years and his mother notices he does not speak like her son anymore. He has spent his tongue — he talks like a clerk at a counter now, in the flattened Hindi he uses for arguing with officials, and he cannot get his own dialect back.

## Open risks (author's reaction pending)
1. The wife's claim must be STAGED as just, not merely asserted, or the audience hears entitlement and the story collapses into a guilt-trip. Hardest writing problem in it.
2. He is kept unlikeable-adjacent on purpose (the sentimental version is unwatchable) — a dial the author may want set differently.
